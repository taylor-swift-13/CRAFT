%% ============================================================
\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

We ask how composition scales with sampling (RQ1), how much compositional
inference and training improve verification (RQ2), how \sam{} compares with
specialized tools (RQ3), how RL reshapes compositional exploration before and
after SFT (RQ4),
and how credit assignment affects compositional exploration under RL (RQ5).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\tightparagraph{Data and judge.}
Training data comprise 4,992 RL records and 33,346 SFT records (31,865
distinct sources) of target-masked scalar-integer single-loop programs;
Appendix~\ref{app:data-curation} gives their synthesis and verification.  SFT
targets and RL rewards are produced only by the open-weight model being trained,
program execution, and Frama-C/WP verification; no external teacher or
closed-model supervision is used.  The
evaluation combines 832 programs from Code2Inv, SyGuS, SV-COMP, LIPuS,
Clause2Inv, and Loopy: 316 linear, 50 nonlinear-arithmetic, and 466 Loopy
instances~\citep{si2020code2inv,alur2013sygus,svcomp,yu2023lipus,
cao2025clause2inv,kamath2023finding}.  No training program matches an
evaluation program under exact, alpha-renamed, or constant-abstracted matching
(Appendix~\ref{app:data-leakage}).  The judge is Frama-C 27.1 (Cobalt) with
the WP plug-in, Typed memory model, and Alt-Ergo/Z3 prover portfolio
~\citep{framac}.  Success requires it to prove induction and every restored
target; all other outcomes count as zero.

\tightparagraph{Inference protocol.}
All \sam{} evaluation runs share target masking, the canonical prompt, clause
processing, Houdini, stochastic decoding, and no re-roll or target feedback;
external tools retain their own target-hidden orchestration.  RQ1 reuses one
saved pool of \(n_{\mathrm{probe}}\) responses per task.  Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol}
gives decoding, serving, prover, and training details.

\tightparagraph{Metrics.}
Throughout evaluation, success means \(\mathcal G\)-sufficiency under the final
verifier.  pass@\(k\) estimates whether any of \(k\) responses succeeds alone;
compose@\(k\) measures whether filtering their clause union succeeds.  The
former uses the standard unbiased estimator, while the latter evaluates saved
response prefixes; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol} gives both
estimators.

\subsection{RQ1: How Do pass@k and compose@k Scale?}
\label{sec:exp:probe}

We sample \(n_{\mathrm{probe}}\) target-hidden responses per task from six base checkpoints:
Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-30B-A3B
~\citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Llama 3.1 8B.  Complete curves and
whole-workload values appear in Appendix~\ref{tab:probe-complete}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{figures/base_model_probe.pdf}
\caption{pass@\(k\) (unbiased estimate) and prefix compose@\(k\).
Composition saturates by \(k{\approx}8\)--\(16\), while pass@\(k\) keeps growing.}
\label{fig:base-model-probe}
\end{figure}

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight.}
Across all six checkpoints, compose@16 is within 2.6 points of compose@32,
while pass@\(k\) keeps growing.  The verification benefit of clause pooling
therefore saturates earlier than the probability of sampling a complete
answer.
\end{insightbox}

We compare \sam{} with the target-hidden pipelines of AutoSpec, SESpec,
Clause2Inv, Loopy, and Daikon~\citep{wen2024enchanting,yang2026sespec,

49.04\% with fewer tokens than AutoSpec, the strongest external baseline in
this reasoning-disabled comparison (42.19\%), while compose@8 reaches
55.53\%.  The comparison is end-to-end rather than call- or token-matched.
The complete per-category accuracy, token, runtime, and reasoning-effort
breakdown appears in Appendix~\ref{app:complete-results}; the benefit also
holds for three additional API models (Table~\ref{tab:cross-model-main}).

\subsection{RQ4: How Does RL Reshape Compositional Exploration Before and After SFT?}
\label{sec:exp:rl}

Prior work suggests that RLVR can reallocate probability toward behaviors
already available to the base model rather than create fundamentally new
reasoning patterns~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}.  We examine this view at the
level of compositional utility through matched Zero--RL-Zero and
SFT--SFT+RL Qwen3-8B pairs under identical inference.  The first comparison
measures how RL changes an untrained policy; the second asks what RL adds
after SFT has distilled multi-response compositions.

Under both initializations, RL makes useful compositional content easier to
sample, but the curves have different shapes.  From Zero, RL raises compose@1
by 20.07 points and compose@32 by 15.39 points, while pass@1 is nearly
unchanged (6.80\% vs.\ 6.43\%).  One RL-Zero rollout reaches 57.93\%
compose@1, slightly exceeding the 57.81\% obtained from 32 Zero rollouts.
The persistent gain at \(k{=}32\) is consistent with sharpening a diffuse
long tail: useful clauses may be too rare under Zero for 32 samples to expose
them reliably.  The finite sweep, however, does not establish whether those
clauses were already present in that tail.

After SFT, the sharpening pattern becomes clearer.  RL raises compose@1 by
5.33 points but compose@32 by only 0.47 points.  Once large-\(k\) exploration
is already effective, RL changes primarily how quickly useful compositional
content is recovered rather than how much is recovered at the largest tested
budget.

This probability shift favors compositional utility rather than complete
responses.  Pass@1 falls from 41.93\% to 30.78\% and pass@32 from 67.98\% to
53.47\%, whereas compose@1 rises from 63.90\% to 69.23\% and compose@32 from
77.90\% to 78.37\%.  The compose@1 gain holds across all four backbones
(2.40--5.33 points), all three benchmark strata, and the controlled clause-cap
sweep (Table~\ref{tab:rcf-paired}; Appendices~\ref{tab:rl-complete}
and~\ref{app:clause-cap}).

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight.}
Across both initializations, RL makes useful compositional content easier to
sample.  From Zero, this lifts the entire measured curve, consistent with
sharpening a diffuse long tail; after SFT, the gains concentrate at small
\(k\), revealing the sharpening pattern more clearly.  RL sharpens
compositional utility, not whole-response correctness.
\end{insightbox}

\centering

  raises small-\(k\) composition after SFT saturates at large \(k\).}

\subsection{RQ5: How Should Credit Be Assigned for Compositional Inference?}
\label{sec:exp:ablations}

At inference, utility is determined only after responses are pooled and
filtered.  Because targets remain hidden during RL, negative coverage of the
pooled survivors serves as a target-independent surrogate.  We test where
this credit should be assigned.  From the same Qwen3-8B Zero initialization,
Binary and Whole-rollout use response-level credit, Clause-decomposed
preserves credit for surviving \(V_i\), and Full adds the group allocation
term \(w_g\phi_i\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:generation-reward}); all other settings
are fixed.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{figures/reward_ablation.pdf}
\caption{Qwen3-8B reward ablation: whole-response credit reduces
  composition; clause and group credit recover it (Appendix~\ref{tab:reward-ablation}).}
\label{fig:reward-ablation}
\end{figure}

Whole-rollout credit raises pass@1 from 6.43\% to 16.67\% yet reduces
compose@32 from 57.81\% to 22.72\%, showing that higher standalone success can
coincide with worse pooled composition.  Clause-decomposed credit reverses
this reduction, improving compose@1 and compose@32 over Zero by 10.22 and 4.09
points; group credit adds another 9.62--11.30 points across \(k\) by rewarding
nonredundant coverage.

We also test how the target-independent surrogate should be constructed by
comparing structured negatives with random perturbations.
Structured negatives add 2.0--5.3 compose points over random perturbations;
their target-hidden coverage predicts restored-target success (pooled AUROC
0.793; within-program 0.802).  On NLA, composition still improves
verification, but the near-chance coverage AUROC of 0.554 shows that the
current target-independent signal cannot reliably rank nonlinear candidates.
Appendices~\ref{app:ablation-definitions} and
\ref{app:coverage-predictiveness} give the full results.

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight.}
When inference builds an answer from reusable parts, training should credit
the parts that survive and discount contributions repeated across the group,
rather than judge each response as a whole.
\end{insightbox}
