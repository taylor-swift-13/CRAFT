%% ============================================================
\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

We ask how composition scales with sampling (RQ1), how much each pipeline
component contributes (RQ2), how \sam{} compares with specialized tools (RQ3),
how much RL adds (RQ4), and
what drives the training gain (RQ5).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\paragraph{Data and judge.}
The sanitized pool contains 37,481 target-masked scalar-integer single-loop
records; the released training sets are curated from it and from
1,392 newly synthesized programs
(Appendix~\ref{app:data-curation}): 5,000 RL records and
14,858 SFT records (11,800 of them with
pipeline-synthesized targets), overlapping on 180 programs.  Evaluation uses the standard
benchmark suites of prior learning-based invariant-generation systems: 832~C
programs, comprising 316 linear programs (133 \textsc{Code2Inv}
\citep{si2020code2inv}, 84 SyGuS 2019 \citep{alur2013sygus}, 99 SV-COMP 2024
\citep{svcomp}) and 50 nonlinear-arithmetic (NLA) programs (30 LIPuS
\citep{yu2023lipus}, 20 SV-COMP 2024) as released with
\textsc{Clause2Inv}~\citep{cao2025clause2inv}, plus 466 integer programs from
Loopy~\citep{kamath2023finding}.  The overlap and prompt-sanitation audits
are in Appendix~\ref{app:data-leakage}.  We remove non-contract comments and
mask all target predicates before generation, then restore the untouched
source only after invariants are fixed.
Success requires Frama-C/WP~\citep{framac}, under a 5-second per-obligation
prover budget, to prove establishment, preservation, and every restored
target; all failures count as zero.

\paragraph{Inference protocol.}
All \sam{} evaluation runs share target masking, the canonical prompt, clause
processing, and Houdini; external tools retain their own target-hidden orchestration.  Within
\sam{}, API models use \texttt{reasoning\_effort=none}; all locally served
checkpoints use non-thinking decoding to match data synthesis and training.  Decoding uses
temperature 1.0 and top-\(p\) 1.0.  API calls cap completions at 8,192 tokens,
including provider-internal reasoning tokens; local vLLM calls cap each
response at 2,048 emitted tokens.  We use no re-roll or target feedback.  RQ1
reuses one saved batch of 100 stochastic responses per task.
Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol} gives benchmark provenance, serving,
and training details.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Throughout evaluation, success means \(Q\)-sufficiency under the final
verifier.  pass@\(k\) is the probability that at least one of \(k\) responses
proves \(Q\).  Whenever a saved pool contains \(c\) successful responses among
\(n=100\), we use the unbiased estimator
\(\widehat{\operatorname{pass@}k}=1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\) for that
program and average it over programs.
Only responses that prove \(Q\) count as successes.  compose@\(k\) is the
fraction of programs proved by unioning the first \(k\) responses and applying
Houdini.  pass@\(k\) requires one response to contain the whole proof, whereas
compose@\(k\) can compose clauses from different responses.  We do not compute
the exact all-subset analogue for compose@\(k\), because it would require
filtering \(\binom{100}{k}\) unions per program; we evaluate the fixed saved
prefix instead.  For the measured budget grid
\(\mathcal K=\{1,10,30,50,100\}\), we report
\(k_{95}=\min\{k\in\mathcal K:
\operatorname{Comp}_\pi(k)\geq0.95\,\operatorname{Comp}_\pi(100)\}\), where
\(\operatorname{Comp}_\pi(k)\) is the measured compose@\(k\) curve.  A smaller
\(k_{95}\) means the retained pool reaches its large-budget performance
sooner.

\subsection{RQ1: How Do pass@k and compose@k Scale?}
\label{sec:exp:probe}

We sample 100 target-hidden responses per task from six base checkpoints:
Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-30B-A3B
~\citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Llama 3.1 8B.  Complete values and \(k_{95}\) appear in
Appendix~\ref{tab:probe-complete}, per-stratum breakdowns in
Table~\ref{tab:probe-stratum}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{figures/base_model_probe.pdf}
\caption{pass@\(k\) (unbiased estimate) and prefix compose@\(k\).
Composition saturates by \(k=30\), while pass@\(k\) keeps growing.}
\label{fig:base-model-probe}
\end{figure}

At \(k=1\), prefix compose@\(k\) is 8.92--25.18 points above the unbiased pass
estimate across checkpoints.  Because these are different estimands rather
than paired first-response outcomes, we treat this gap as descriptive; RQ2
reports the paired effect of processing the same response.  In the retained
response pools, every model
reaches 95\% of compose@100 by \(k=50\), with at most 1.56 additional points
through \(k=100\).  In contrast, pass@30 reaches only 59.6--88.8\% of
pass@100 on the five Qwen checkpoints and gains another 1.46--4.66 points by
\(k=100\).  Llama 3.1 8B is harder in standalone proof generation---pass@100
is 10.46\%---but shows the same compositional pattern: compose rises from
18.03\% at \(k=1\) to 28.25\% at \(k=30\), then changes by only 0.12 points.
Composition thus
finds most of its useful clauses within the first few dozen samples,
whereas standalone proofs require wider sampling.
The compose@100 values (28.37--39.78\%) further suggest that these pools
remain limited by clause quality or diversity: extending the budget to
100 responses does not remove the gap.

\begin{tcolorbox}[
  colback=algobg,
  colframe=algoframe,
  boxrule=0.6pt,
  arc=2pt,
  left=5pt,right=5pt,top=4pt,bottom=4pt
]
\textbf{Insight.} pass@1 is not an appropriate primary metric for
compositional invariant generation.  By treating a response as indivisible,
it assigns zero both to useless outputs and to incomplete outputs containing
valid, reusable clauses.  It remains a standalone diagnostic, whereas
compose@\(k\) directly measures whether clauses found within the inference
budget form a sufficient invariant after filtering.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{RQ3: How Does \sam{} Compare with Specialized Tools?}
\label{sec:exp:tools}

We compare against the target-hidden pipelines of
\textsc{AutoSpec}, \textsc{SESpec}, and \textsc{Clause2Inv}, and against
Daikon 5.8.24
~\citep{wen2024enchanting,yang2026sespec,cao2025clause2inv,daikon}.
All tool rows use \texttt{gpt-5-nano} where they call an LLM.  The Naive row
measures the model's unaided behavior: one call with no invariant-specific
instructions, under which the model emits only the few clauses it considers
safe.  \sam{}'s prompt instead adapts the model to the pipeline, asking for
up to twenty clauses that cover conservation laws, progress bounds, and
exit facts.  This trades single-response reliability for clause coverage:
pass@1 judges all clauses in a response as an indivisible conjunction, so
one invalid or non-inductive clause fails the whole response, and \sam{}'s
richer raw outputs score below Naive before filtering (3.61\% vs.\
16.47\%).  The gap is by design---the discarded clauses carry no cost,
because the pipeline keeps only what survives filtering, which is what
compose@1 measures.
\textsc{Clause2Inv}'s unsupported inputs remain failures in the common
832-program denominator.  Budgets are fixed before comparison but not matched:
each system retains its predeclared native orchestration.

reasoning-enabled comparison is retained in

\paragraph{Failure modes under target hiding.}
The specialized systems degrade for different structural reasons.  In the
evaluated one-round configuration, \textsc{AutoSpec} requests eight
completions in parallel, pools their clauses, and verifier-filters the union;
it is therefore already an eight-proposal composition method rather than a
single-proposal baseline.  Nevertheless, it verifies 351 programs, 23 fewer
than \sam{} compose@1.
Its extra samples mainly add redundant or weak clauses rather than the
complementary facts recovered by \sam{}'s clause processing.  \textsc{SESpec}
instead makes 5.09 sequential calls per completed task on average.  This
refinement-heavy schedule does not provide the
independent parallel exploration needed when the target is absent; moreover,
errors from the target-hidden verifier diagnose syntax and inductiveness, not
which missing relation would establish the hidden goal.  \textsc{Clause2Inv}'s
ICE loop normally uses false-state (\texttt{F:}) counterexamples induced by the
postcondition.  Target-hidden execution suppresses the postcondition check and
retains only pre-state and transition counterexamples, removing its principal
goal-directed feedback signal; its required transition VCs are also
unavailable for all 466 Loopy programs.

\textsc{Loopy} exhibits a separate interface failure.  Its extractor accepts
only the first valid Markdown-fenced code block.  Although 98.8\% of the
non-reasoning initial responses contain textual \texttt{loop invariant}
clauses, only 1.75\% use the required fence and reach Houdini; for repair
responses the corresponding rates are 94.5\% and 1.13\%.  Houdini can prune
bad clauses but cannot recover discarded ones.  Target hiding further removes
the assertion-specific checker diagnostic expected by Loopy's repair prompt,
leaving only generic inductiveness feedback: repair adds just 15 successes to
the 141 initially solved tasks, for 156/832 (18.75\%) overall.  Thus this row
primarily measures a brittle parser and a repair protocol mismatched to the
hidden-target setting, rather than the quality of the invariants present in
the raw responses.  The archived medium-reasoning run is reported separately
in the appendix.

\textbf{Finding.} Target visibility is a problem boundary, not an
implementation detail.  Goal-directed feedback---\textsc{Clause2Inv}'s
postcondition-induced counterexamples, \textsc{Loopy}'s assertion-specific
diagnostics---vanishes once the target is hidden, and the two systems fall
to 7.93\% and 18.75\%; five target-hidden responses composed by \sam{}
verify 61.06\%.  Under target hiding, complementary composition survives
where iterative goal-directed repair does not.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{RQ4: How Much Does Reinforcement Learning Improve Inference?}
\label{sec:exp:rl}

We evaluate matched Qwen3-8B before--after RL paths from base and SFT
initializations.  Inference is fixed within each pair; RQ1 is not used to
estimate the RL effect.

\paragraph{What to expect.}
If RL with whole-response credit merely sharpens the
distribution~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}, it should raise pass@1 while leaving
or degrading large-\(k\) coverage.  Clause-decomposed, group-compositional
credit predicts the opposite pattern: pass@\(k\) stays flat, while
compose@\(k\) shifts up at small budgets without degrading at large ones.

The SFT stage distills the filtered multi-response union into single
responses (Section~\ref{sec:method:sft}).  On Qwen3-8B it doubles
pass@1 (3.55\%$\to$7.10\%) and lifts compose@10 from 36.78\% to
39.06\%, while compose@1 stays flat (26.32\%$\to$25.60\%): the
distilled clauses help once several responses pool, not in
isolation (Table~\ref{tab:rl-before-after}); full SFT curves across
four backbones appear in Appendix~\ref{app:sft-probe}.

\paragraph{Results.}
Consistent with the redistribution view, response-level accuracy does not
improve after RL (Figure~\ref{fig:rl-panels}, left; pass@1 TBD vs.\ TBD).
The redistribution is instead steered toward the complementary tail of the
clause pool: compose@\(k^{*}\) with \(k^{*}{=}\)TBD matches its
initialization's compose@100 (TBD), an effective TBD-fold budget
reduction,
and compose@100 itself does not degrade (TBD vs.\ TBD).  RL thus
packs into \(k^{*}\) samples the useful clauses its initialization
produces only across one hundred.  A
clause-frequency analysis against TBD initialization samples attributes part
of the gain to clauses the initialization produces with probability below TBD
per response.

\subsection{RQ5: What Drives the Training Gain?}
\label{sec:exp:ablations}

Both ablations use Qwen3-8B with all other training and inference settings
fixed.  The reward ablation reports three matched seeds; the negative-sampler
ablation compares matched runs after 200 training steps.
Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-definitions} gives the exact reward definitions
and per-seed reward results.

\paragraph{Reward-function ablation.}
Variants progress from binary inductiveness to whole-response coverage, then
credit surviving clauses individually, add Shapley credit, and penalize
duplicate clauses.  All use a
20-clause cap; coverage variants share the overflow cost, while the binary
control remains \(\{0,1\}\).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{figures/reward_ablation.pdf}
\caption{Reward-function ablation on Qwen3-8B (three-seed means).
  Binary and whole-rollout credit raise response-level accuracy at small
  budgets (b) but flatten compositional coverage (a).  Clause decomposition
  reverses this trade-off, and Shapley credit shifts the compose curve further
  toward the upper left: both dominate the response-level variants at every
  $k$ in (a), and
  Shapley credit also ends above the untrained model at $k{=}100$ (41.11\%
  vs.\ 39.78\%).
  Exact numbers for all variants appear in
  Table~\ref{tab:reward-ablation}
  (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-definitions}).}
\label{fig:reward-ablation}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:reward-ablation} reveals
two effects (exact numbers in Table~\ref{tab:reward-ablation},
Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-definitions}).  \emph{First, binary inductiveness collapses composition.}
Although its pass@1 reaches 8.65\%, the policy degenerates into repeating a
few safe clauses, so compose@100 stalls at 12.02\%, 27.8 points below the
untrained model.  This is the sampling-limit failure mode anticipated in
Section~\ref{sec:exp:rl}: whole-response RLVR sharpens the policy toward a
small set of already-supported complete responses.  It raises pass@1, but
its advantage vanishes with sampling---at $k{=}100$, both response-level
variants reach only 13.46\% pass@100, below the untrained model's 15.02\%---and
their compose curves saturate early.
\emph{Second, credit must be decomposed to clauses.}  Moving from
whole-rollout to clause-decomposed coverage raises compose@100 from 26.56\%
to 39.06\%, and Shapley credit adds a further +2.05 points (41.11\%):
rewarding complementary coverage across the group translates directly into
composable invariants, at the price of fragmenting individual responses
(pass@1 drops to 2.31\%).

\paragraph{Breaking the response-level sampling limit.}
Why does credit structure decide the outcome?  Whole-response rewards
grade each rollout by its own clauses alone, so they can only push
probability mass toward responses the policy already favors---the
sharpening dynamic that confines per-sample RL to the support of its
initialization~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}.  Shapley credit instead
grades each rollout against its peers: a rejected negative earns more
when fewer peers reject it.  The objective is the group's union rather
than any single response, and the measured effect is the opposite of
sharpening: once whole-response correctness is removed from the objective,
pass@1 falls below the untrained model (2.31\% vs.\
3.55\%) while compose@100 rises above it (41.11\% vs.\ 39.78\%).
The same RL procedure can therefore collapse compose@100
(binary credit, $-27.8$ points), preserve it (clause decomposition),
or raise it (Shapley credit).  What RL does to the sampling
distribution is decided by what the reward values, not by RL itself.

\textbf{Finding (RLVR limits).}  Response-level RLVR sharpens rather than
expands the sampled support: it improves pass@1 but falls below the untrained
pass curve at large $k$, so additional sampling cannot break the limit.
Shapley diversity delays saturation and shifts compositional coverage toward
the upper left, but the decisive change is clause decomposition.  By no
longer requiring an entire response to be correct, it supplies denser credit:
pass@1 drops from 10.80\% to 2.66\%, while compose@100 rises from 26.56\% to
39.06\% (and to 41.11\% with Shapley credit).
\end{tcolorbox}
