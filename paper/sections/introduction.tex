\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Loop invariants are the linchpin of deductive program verification.
They summarize an unbounded computation in a finite assertion, connecting the
operational behavior of a loop to the mathematical facts needed by a proof.
Generating them therefore demands both \emph{program understanding}---tracking
control flow, assignments, and interacting recurrences---and
\emph{mathematical reasoning}---discovering relations and bounds that hold
across arbitrarily many iterations.
Ever since the inductive-assertion method of Floyd~\citep{floyd1967assigning}
and the axiomatic semantics of Hoare~\citep{hoare1969axiomatic}, verifying a
loop $\mathtt{while}\,(B)\,S$ against a contract $\{P\}\cdot\{Q\}$ has reduced
to exhibiting an \emph{inductive invariant} $I$ satisfying initiation
($P \Rightarrow I$), consecution ($\{I \wedge B\}\,S\,\{I\}$), and safety
($I \wedge \neg B \Rightarrow Q$).
The goal is not merely to find a predicate that satisfies the first two obligations.
An invariant such as $\mathit{true}$ may be inductive but carries no information to the loop exit.
Useful loop reasoning must preserve enough information to establish the required post-loop condition $Q$.
This final obligation is the distinction between generating arbitrary invariants and completing loop verification.

When the desired post-loop condition \(Q\) is available, invariant synthesis
can exploit it directly as a search objective.
Template- and constraint-based procedures, counterexample-guided inductive
synthesis (CEGIS), ICE-style learners, and postcondition-guided inference can
use \(Q\), failed proof obligations, or counterexamples to steer the search
toward an invariant sufficient for \(Q\)
~\citep{gulwani2008program,gupta2009invgen,dillig2013inductive,
alur2013sygus,garg2014ice,garg2016learning,nguyen2017syminfer,
furia2010inferring}.
Recent systems further combine this verifier-guided loop with neural or
large language model (LLM) proposals, filtering, ranking, and repair
~\citep{si2020code2inv,kamath2023finding,chakraborty2023ranking,
wu2024lam4inv,cao2025clause2inv,liu2024acinv}.
However, these formulations presuppose that \(Q\) has already been written,
whereas obtaining a useful post-loop condition is often itself a nontrivial
specification task.
For a \texttt{while} loop, an invariant naturally induces such an exit
condition when combined with the negated guard.
We therefore reverse the usual direction: without exposing the ground-truth
target \(Q\), we generate an invariant \(I\) from the loop and define its
induced post-loop condition as
\[
  Q' \triangleq I \wedge \neg B.
\]
We then evaluate whether the generated condition is strong enough by checking
\(Q' \Rightarrow Q\), using \(Q\) only as held-out ground truth rather than as
input to synthesis.

Checking a proposed invariant and discovering one are markedly different
problems.
Once a candidate is fixed, an automated theorem prover (ATP) can often discharge
its verification conditions.
Generation must first invent the right abstraction from an open-ended space of
predicates: it must be weak enough to hold initially and survive every
iteration, yet strong enough to imply a downstream property.
This tension has made automatic loop-invariant generation a longstanding and
difficult problem.

\paragraph{Why numerical loops?}
We focus on numerical loops for two reasons.
First, candidate invariants in this domain are predominantly arithmetic
equalities and inequalities, often polynomial ones.
Their correctness is comparatively amenable to automatic checking, so ATP
feedback can serve as a scalable and objective signal without requiring the
interactive proofs commonly needed for richer program states.
Second, numerical loops largely factor out the representational machinery of
arrays, heaps, and recursive data structures, whose verification often depends
on inventing auxiliary functions or inductive predicates.
They therefore provide a controlled setting in which success depends more
directly on algebraic discovery and reasoning about loop dynamics, rather than
on reproducing a particular data-structure abstraction.

This restriction does not make the problem easy.
The Collatz iteration, for example, is expressible as a one-variable loop with
two elementary arithmetic updates, yet whether it reaches \(1\) from every
positive integer remains open~\citep{lagarias1985collatz}.
Consequently, no known polynomial invariant, together with an automatically
checkable descent argument, suffices to certify this global claim; such a
certificate would resolve the conjecture.
Even within this deliberately simple domain, compact program syntax can thus
hide mathematical behavior beyond presently known invariants.

\paragraph{From algorithmic to learned generation.}
Classical algorithmic methods have made their strongest exact progress on
affine transition systems.
Karr's seminal procedure computes affine equalities, while abstract
interpretation and constraint-based methods extend this foundation to linear
equalities and inequalities
~\citep{karr1976affine,cousot1977abstract,cousot1978automatic,colon2003linear}.
The canonical setting is a loop whose numerical state \(\mathbf{y}\) evolves as
\[
  \mathbf{y}' = A\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{b},
\]
possibly with linear guards.
Within this setting, invariant inference can exploit linear algebra, convex
geometry, or decidable constraint solving.
Methods based on polynomial templates and Gr\"obner bases extend the frontier
to selected nonlinear algebraic invariants, but impose syntactic or algebraic
restrictions and face an explicit trade-off between completeness and
tractability~\citep{sankaranarayanan2004non}.
Thus, the most robust algorithmic guarantees remain concentrated around affine
updates, rather than general nonlinear loop dynamics.

Data-driven and learned methods broaden the search by using traces,
counterexamples, or neural generators
~\citep{garg2016learning,si2020code2inv,kamath2023finding,cao2025clause2inv}.
Many such systems expose $Q$ to the generator and use its proof as feedback.
This is effective for a fixed verification instance, but it creates a shortcut:
a model can tailor candidates to the visible assertion without learning the
relations and bounds that explain the loop.
Postcondition-derived invariant mutation makes this dependence
explicit~\citep{furia2010inferring}.

\paragraph{Motivating example.}
Consider \texttt{NLA\_lipus/10.c}, abridged below:
\begin{lstlisting}
int x = 1, y = 1, c = 1;
while (c < k) {
  c = c + 1;
  x = x*z + 1;
  y = y*z;
}
/*@ assert 1+x*z-x-z*y == 0; */
\end{lstlisting}
The assertion itself is established by the initial state and preserved by the loop body.
When it is visible, invariant generation collapses to copying the target:
\texttt{loop invariant 1+x*z-x-z*y == 0;}.
The resulting clause is inductive and proves the assertion without revealing why this relation follows from the two coupled recurrences.
When the assertion is hidden, the same clause must instead be recovered from the assignments to \texttt{x} and \texttt{y}.
This small example captures the distinction we study: target-visible verification can make invariant inference trivial even when the underlying relation is nonlinear.

\paragraph{Target-hidden loop verification.}
We study a stricter setting.
The full verification instance contains a precondition, loop, and post-loop target $Q$, but the policy receives only the precondition, guard, body, and in-scope variables.
Assertions and \texttt{ensures} clauses are removed before generation, refinement, and Houdini pruning.
The policy emits ACSL loop invariants, not a new postcondition.
Only at the final gate does \sam{} restore the original target and ask Frama-C/WP to prove all invariant obligations and the post-loop condition.
For a standard \texttt{while} loop, acceptance therefore requires
\[
P \Rightarrow I,\qquad
\{I \wedge B\}\,S\,\{I\},\qquad
I \wedge \neg B \Rightarrow Q.
\]
The hidden target determines success, while the generator cannot read or paraphrase it.
The task is consequently not ``generate any loop invariant,'' but ``recover loop conditions that carry enough information to the exit to prove a held-out requirement.''

\paragraph{One framework for training and inference.}
The central design of \sam{} is a shared rollout--Houdini loop, rather than separate training and deployment procedures.
Given the masked program, both paths sample a group of invariant rollouts, normalize and merge their clauses, optionally grow the pool through stateless WP-guided repairs, and invoke the same Houdini-style Frama-C/WP operator~\citep{flanagan2001houdini,framac} to retain an inductive subset.
Only the consumer of those survivors changes.
During training, Houdini survivors are scored for behavioral coverage:
generation rollouts receive standalone credit plus extra reward for hard trace
units rejected by few group peers, and repairs receive credit for improving the
merged pool.  Malformed or non-inductive clauses are removed without erasing
the value of usable clauses in the same response.  A unique clause with zero
sampled coverage is not penalized because it may support another survivor or
the hidden target; only conservative semantic duplicates incur the redundancy
cost.
During inference, the final Houdini survivors are inserted into the original program and checked against the restored \(Q\).
Thus reward is coupled to exactly the rollout, pool-growth, feedback, and pruning semantics used at deployment; it is not an auxiliary objective attached to a different inference pipeline.

Hiding $Q$ removes the binary target-verification reward, while inductiveness alone remains too weak because tautologies and loose bounds pass.
The execution sampler therefore constructs relational perturbations, continuations beyond a genuine exit, and sampled-range escapes without consulting \(Q\).
Candidates contradicted by observed reachability, feasible fresh entries, or incomplete state coverage are suppressed.
These audited traces provide the dense strength signal attached to the shared framework, while the held-out target remains the authoritative final criterion.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{A target-hidden verification task.} We separate the policy view from the verification instance: the model generates invariants without $Q$, while acceptance still requires proving $Q$ after the loop ($\S$\ref{sec:overview}).
  \item \textbf{A shared rollout--Houdini framework.} Training and inference use the same grouped rollouts, growing clause pools, stateless WP feedback, and prover-backed Houdini survivors; training attaches rewards, while inference attaches the restored final proof obligation.
  \item \textbf{An execution-grounded objective.} Generation receives
  standalone negative coverage and positive hardness credit for trace units
  rejected by few group peers; refinement receives its gain over the shared
  pool.  Response caps and conservative duplicate/overflow costs prevent clause
  accumulation, while three conservative negative families provide target-free
  strength evidence (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{An auditable evaluation contract.} We define controlled
  target-visibility, equal-budget inference, and reward ablations, and validate
  the implemented signal with known-quality discrimination and a full-suite
  label audit (\S\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}
