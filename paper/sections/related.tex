\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{Programmatic invariant synthesis.}
Classical methods infer invariants with abstract interpretation, affine
equalities, templates, interpolation, recurrences, or constraint solving
~\citep{karr1976affine,cousot1977abstract,cousot1978automatic,
sankaranarayanan2004non,colon2003linear,mcmillan2003interpolation,
kincaid2017compositional}.  These methods provide strong guarantees for
selected languages, especially affine transition systems, but nonlinear
templates trade generality for increasingly difficult algebraic search.
More generally, proving inductiveness certifies that an invariant is a sound
over-approximation of reachable loop states, but does not say how precise that
over-approximation is.  Many weak invariants are therefore correct, while a
strongest invariant may be unavailable or inexpressible in the chosen
language.  Dynamic approaches infer likely relations from execution traces and
then filter or prove them~\citep{daikon,nguyen2012using,garg2016learning}.
\sam{} instead uses executions to construct a graded, target-independent
training signal for precision; final soundness is still established
independently by the verifier.
Target-directed software model checkers constitute a related but distinct
setting.  For example, \textsc{Ultimate Automizer} (UAuto) uses trace
abstraction and interpolation to verify a supplied safety property
~\citep{heizmann2013automizer}.  Although its correctness witnesses may contain
inductive assertions, they are synthesized with the verification target
visible.  Our setting withholds that target until the candidate set is fixed,
so neither goal-directed refinement nor target counterexamples can provide the
strength signal.

\paragraph{Neural and LLM-based invariant generation.}
Learned methods broaden the candidate language by using neural generators,
counterexamples, or verifier feedback
~\citep{si2018learning,si2020code2inv,ryan2020cln2inv,yao2020gcln}.
In particular, \textsc{Code2Inv} learns a sequential invariant-construction
policy from sparse verifier reward~\citep{si2018learning,si2020code2inv}.
\textsc{Loopy} generates candidate clauses, filters them with Houdini, and
repairs failures using verifier feedback~\citep{kamath2023finding}.
\textsc{Clause2Inv} iteratively combines clauses using historical
counterexamples and failed checks to guide later generation, while
\textsc{iRank} learns to rank LLM-generated invariants before expensive
verifier calls~\citep{cao2025clause2inv,chakraborty2023ranking}.
Thus, clause generation, combination, Houdini filtering, and learned invariant
policies are not individually new.  Unlike counterexample-guided iterative
repair, our inference procedure receives no feedback between rollouts: it
samples a fixed group once and applies union and Houdini only after generation.
The target remains hidden throughout generation and training.

\paragraph{Specification generation.}
LLM-based tools also synthesize broader program specifications.
\textsc{AutoSpec} repeatedly proposes and verifies annotations;
\textsc{SpecGen} combines LLM proposals with mutation in Java/JML; and
\textsc{SESpec} uses symbolic execution to generate goal-independent
specifications for C
~\citep{wen2024enchanting,ma2024specgen,yang2026sespec}.
We study only loop invariants and ask whether they prove a held-out target,
rather than evaluating a general behavioral specification.  This restriction
exposes a useful compositional structure: invariant responses can be split into
clauses, verified independently or jointly, and recombined without asking a
model to merge natural-language rationales or rewrite a complete contract.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning from formal feedback.}
Verifier-guided reinforcement learning has been used for program synthesis and
formal reasoning.  \textsc{Re:Form} uses scalable curation and verifier-guided
RL for Dafny, while \textsc{SpecRL} generates impossible input--output pairs
and rewards specifications that reject them
~\citep{yan2025reform,huang2026specrl}.
Validity alone does not distinguish a useful specification from a weak one.
\textsc{SpecRL} is closest to our negative-example signal: it measures the
strength of each generated specification by the impossible behaviors that the
specification excludes.  Its negative examples are generic method-level
input--output pairs: the model proposes inputs, execution supplies outputs,
and output mutation constructs impossible pairs.  Our sampler instead targets
loop-head state space with relational perturbations, post-exit continuations,
and range perturbations.  Moreover, \textsc{SpecRL} scores a complete generated
specification independently.  We instead compute rewards jointly across a
rollout group: one rollout's credit depends on the verified behaviors already
covered by its peers.  We also use verification to retain and reward a sound
clause subset inside an imperfect response, rather than assigning correctness
to the response as one indivisible object.  \textsc{SpecRL} does not split loop
invariants into clauses or optimize the cross-rollout union selected by
Houdini.

Verifiable-reward RL can improve small-budget accuracy while reducing the
coverage available from extensive sampling~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}.  This
tension also motivates evaluating both standalone and multi-sample coverage.
Our method-level response is described in Section~\ref{sec:method}: SFT and RL
are trained around the same fixed-group union-and-Houdini operator used at
deployment, rather than extending a standalone verifier reward.
