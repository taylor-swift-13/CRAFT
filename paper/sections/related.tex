\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\paragraph{Programmatic invariant synthesis.}
Classical methods infer invariants with abstract interpretation, affine
equalities, templates, interpolation, recurrences, or constraint solving
~\citep{karr1976affine,cousot1977abstract,cousot1978automatic,
sankaranarayanan2004non,colon2003linear,mcmillan2003interpolation,
kincaid2017compositional}.  These methods provide strong guarantees for
selected languages, especially affine transition systems, but nonlinear
templates trade generality for increasingly difficult algebraic search.
Dynamic approaches instead infer likely relations from execution traces and
then filter or prove them~\citep{daikon,nguyen2012using,garg2016learning}.
\sam{} also uses executions, but only to construct a training signal; final
soundness is established independently by the verifier.
Target-directed software model checkers constitute a related but distinct
setting.  For example, \textsc{Ultimate Automizer} (UAuto) uses trace
abstraction and interpolation to verify a supplied safety property
~\citep{heizmann2013automizer}.  Although its correctness witnesses may contain
inductive assertions, they are synthesized with the verification target
visible.  UAuto therefore does not address our target-hidden setting, in which
the assertion is withheld throughout invariant generation, and we do not
include it in the experimental comparison.

\paragraph{Neural and LLM-based invariant generation.}
Learned methods broaden the candidate language by using neural generators,
counterexamples, or verifier feedback
~\citep{si2018learning,si2020code2inv,ryan2020cln2inv,yao2020gcln}.
\textsc{Loopy} generates candidate clauses, filters them with Houdini, and
repairs failures using verifier feedback~\citep{kamath2023finding}.
\textsc{Clause2Inv} combines generated clauses under counterexample guidance,
and \textsc{iRank} learns to rank LLM-generated invariants before expensive
verifier calls~\citep{cao2025clause2inv,chakraborty2023ranking}.
These systems solve a supplied target and commonly evaluate whether one
response proves it.  We hide the target during generation and training, and
train for the filtered combination of compatible clauses from multiple
responses.

\paragraph{Specification generation.}
LLM-based tools also synthesize broader program specifications.
\textsc{AutoSpec} repeatedly proposes and verifies annotations;
\textsc{SpecGen} combines LLM proposals with mutation in Java/JML; and
\textsc{SESpec} uses symbolic execution to generate goal-independent
specifications for C
~\citep{wen2024enchanting,ma2024specgen,yang2026sespec}.
We study only loop invariants and ask whether they prove a held-out target,
rather than evaluating a general behavioral specification.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning from formal feedback.}
Verifier-guided reinforcement learning has been used for program synthesis and
formal reasoning.  \textsc{Re:Form} uses scalable curation and verifier-guided
RL for Dafny, while \textsc{SpecRL} generates impossible input--output pairs
and rewards specifications that reject them
~\citep{yan2025reform,huang2026specrl}.
Validity alone does not distinguish a useful specification from a weak one.
We specialize this idea to loops: synthetic negative states measure strength,
while the verifier proves inductiveness.  Verifiable-reward RL can also improve
small-budget accuracy while narrowing extensive-sampling coverage
~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}.  Because deployment unions samples, our reward adds
group-relative credit for less common behavior that survives Houdini.
