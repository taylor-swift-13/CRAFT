%% ============================================================
\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

We ask how composition scales with sampling (RQ1), how much \sam{} improves
compose@$k$ over standalone responses (RQ2), how \sam{} compares with specialized tools (RQ3),
whether RL still improves small-budget generation before and after SFT (RQ4), and
what prevents compositional exploration from collapsing under RL (RQ5).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\tightparagraph{Data and judge.}
Training data comprise 4,992 RL records and 33,346 SFT records (31,865
distinct sources) of target-masked scalar-integer single-loop programs;
Appendix~\ref{app:data-curation} gives their synthesis and verification.  SFT
targets and RL rewards are produced only by the open-weight model being trained,
program execution, and Frama-C/WP verification; no external teacher or
closed-model supervision is used.  The
evaluation combines 832 programs from Code2Inv, SyGuS, SV-COMP, LIPuS,
Clause2Inv, and Loopy: 316 linear, 50 nonlinear-arithmetic, and 466 Loopy
instances~\citep{si2020code2inv,alur2013sygus,svcomp,yu2023lipus,
cao2025clause2inv,kamath2023finding}.  No training program matches an
evaluation program under exact, alpha-renamed, or constant-abstracted matching
(Appendix~\ref{app:data-leakage}).  The judge is Frama-C 27.1 (Cobalt) with
the WP plug-in, Typed memory model, and Alt-Ergo/Z3 prover portfolio
~\citep{framac}.  Success requires it to prove induction and every restored
target; all other outcomes count as zero.

\tightparagraph{Inference protocol.}
All \sam{} evaluation runs share target masking, the canonical prompt, clause
processing, Houdini, stochastic decoding, and no re-roll or target feedback;
external tools retain their own target-hidden orchestration.  RQ1 reuses one
saved pool of \(n_{\mathrm{probe}}\) responses per task.  Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol}
gives decoding, serving, prover, and training details.

\tightparagraph{Metrics.}
Throughout evaluation, success means \(\mathcal G\)-sufficiency under the final
verifier.  pass@\(k\) estimates whether any of \(k\) responses succeeds alone;
compose@\(k\) measures whether filtering their clause union succeeds.  The
former uses the standard unbiased estimator, while the latter evaluates saved
response prefixes; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol} gives both
estimators.

\subsection{RQ1: How Do pass@k and compose@k Scale?}
\label{sec:exp:probe}

We sample \(n_{\mathrm{probe}}\) target-hidden responses per task from six base checkpoints:
Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-30B-A3B
~\citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Llama 3.1 8B.  Complete curves and
whole-workload values appear in Appendix~\ref{tab:probe-complete}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{figures/base_model_probe.pdf}
\caption{pass@\(k\) (unbiased estimate) and prefix compose@\(k\).
Composition saturates by \(k{\approx}8\)--\(16\), while pass@\(k\) keeps growing.}
\label{fig:base-model-probe}
\end{figure}

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight.}
Across all six checkpoints, compose@16 is within 2.6 points of compose@32,
while pass@\(k\) keeps growing.  Composition therefore recovers most reusable
clauses from the first dozen samples, even when standalone accuracy is low;
nominal model size alone does not determine this gain.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{RQ3: How Does \sam{} Compare with Specialized Tools?}
\label{sec:exp:tools}

We compare \sam{} with the target-hidden pipelines of AutoSpec, SESpec,
Clause2Inv, Loopy, and Daikon~\citep{wen2024enchanting,yang2026sespec,
cao2025clause2inv,kamath2023finding,daikon}.  All LLM-based systems use
GPT-5-nano, all outputs face the same untouched Frama-C/WP judge, and
unsupported inputs count as failures among all 832 programs.  At compose@4,
\sam{} verifies 49.04\%, 6.85 points above the strongest external baseline,
AutoSpec (42.19\%); compose@8 reaches 55.53\%.  This is an end-to-end
comparison under each system's native orchestration, so costs differ.  The
complete accuracy, token, runtime, and reasoning-effort breakdown appears in
Appendix~\ref{tab:tools-complete}; the gain transfers to three further API
models (Appendix~\ref{tab:cross-model-main}).

\subsection{RQ4: Does RL Still Improve Generation after SFT?}
\label{sec:exp:rl}

Decompose--compose inference benefits from multiple responses because useful
clauses can be discovered separately and combined.  We ask whether training
can make these gains available with fewer inference-time samples.  SFT learns
from composed results found by offline exploration; RL then increases the
probability that individual rollouts contribute useful, nonredundant clauses.
We compare matched Zero--RL-Zero and SFT--SFT+RL Qwen3-8B pairs under the same
inference procedure and sampling budgets.

From Zero, RL raises compose@1 by 20.07 points and compose@32 by 15.39 points,
showing improvements at both small and large sampling budgets; pass@1 stays
nearly unchanged (6.80\% vs.\ 6.43\%).  After SFT, RL raises compose@1 by
5.33 points but compose@32 by only 0.47 points.  SFT has therefore already
captured most of the large-budget exploration gain, yet RL still makes more
of that capability available in a single rollout.  The compose@1 gain also
holds on all four trained backbones
(2.40--5.33 points) and all three benchmark strata (Table~\ref{tab:rcf-paired};
full curves in Appendix~\ref{tab:rl-complete}).

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight.}
SFT and RL both transfer the benefits of multi-response exploration to fewer
inference-time samples.  Even after SFT nearly saturates performance at large
\(k\), RL further improves compose@1 by increasing the chance that a single
rollout contributes useful clauses.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{RQ5: Why Does Outcome-Level RL Collapse Compositional Exploration?}
\label{sec:exp:ablations}

Decompose--compose inference optimizes the set utility
\(F(S)=\operatorname{cov}(\operatorname{Houdini}(\bigcup_{i\in S}C_i))\),
not a sum of independent rollout verdicts.  We test whether atomic RLVR
credit therefore conflicts with the algebra of inference.  From the same
Qwen3-8B Zero initialization, Binary and Whole-rollout use response-level
credit, Clause-decomposed preserves credit for surviving \(V_i\), and Full
adds the group marginal term \(w_g\phi_i\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:generation-reward});
all other settings are fixed.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/reward_ablation.pdf}
\caption{Qwen3-8B reward ablation.  Atomic credit collapses composition;
  decomposed credit recovers it, and group credit is best.  Exact values in
  Appendix~\ref{tab:reward-ablation}.}
\label{fig:reward-ablation}
\end{figure}

Whole-rollout credit raises pass@1 from 6.43\% to 16.67\% yet reduces
compose@32 from 57.81\% to 22.72\%: standalone mode concentration destroys
partial support.  Clause-decomposed credit reverses the collapse, improving
compose@1 and compose@32 over Zero by 10.22 and 4.09 points; group credit adds
another 9.85--11.30 points by rewarding nonredundant coverage.

Structured negatives further improve compose@\(k\) by 2.0--5.3 points over
random perturbations.  Their target-hidden coverage predicts restored-target
success (pooled AUROC 0.738; within-program AUROC 0.794), though less reliably
on NLA; Appendices~\ref{app:ablation-definitions} and
\ref{app:coverage-predictiveness} give full results.

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight.}
Atomic credit optimizes a mode; compositional credit optimizes a portfolio.
The unit of training credit must match the algebra of inference.
\end{insightbox}
