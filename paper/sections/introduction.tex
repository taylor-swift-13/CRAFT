\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Loop invariants summarize loop behavior and reduce unbounded executions to
finite proof obligations.  However, target-driven invariant generation
assumes a given postcondition or assertion, which may be absent from legacy
code.  Even when a final
postcondition exists, weakest-precondition reasoning through subsequent loops
and function calls typically relies on intermediate invariants and contracts that are
difficult to discover automatically.  Moreover, invariants tailored to a
particular downstream goal may be less reusable for subsequent analyses with
different objectives.  We therefore study \emph{target-hidden loop
verification}, generating loop properties from the loop context without
exposing a particular verification target.

Existing learning-based approaches, including Code2Inv, CLN2Inv, and
LIPuS~\citep{si2020code2inv,ryan2020cln2inv,yu2023lipus}, and LLM-based
methods such as LaM4Inv, Clause2Inv, and
LORIS~\citep{wu2024lam4inv,cao2025clause2inv,li2026loris}, often use a
given verification target to guide invariant generation and refinement.
With a visible target, whether a candidate invariant helps prove the desired
property provides direct feedback for generation and refinement; when the
target is hidden, this feedback is unavailable.  Inductiveness alone cannot
distinguish useful candidates, since \(\mathsf{true}\) is an invariant of
every loop.  A useful invariant should cover reachable loop states while
excluding as many unreachable states as possible.  Because the exact
reachable set is generally unknown and difficult to express precisely as a
logical formula, we need a computable, target-independent proxy for invariant
strength.

An LLM response may contain both useful and incorrect clauses.  Accepting or
rejecting it as a whole can discard its useful parts.  Responses must therefore
be decomposed into
clauses so their useful parts can inform both inference and training.  Useful
clauses may also be scattered across responses: conjoining valid invariants
can yield a stronger invariant, while candidate clauses that fail preservation
checks in isolation may become jointly inductive with supporting constraints.
Under target hiding, where strength feedback is scarce, pooling and composing
clauses across responses helps build stronger invariants.

We introduce \sam{} to align inference and training with the same
compositional verification procedure.  At inference, it decomposes responses
into clauses, pools them for joint verification, repeatedly removes unproved
clauses, and conjoins the survivors.  During training, we construct candidate
negative traces by perturbing observed states and continuing execution beyond
observed loop exits.  The rate at which verified invariants reject these
traces provides a computable, target-independent proxy for strength, rather
than an exact measure of reachable-set precision.  SFT distills verified
multi-response compositions that meet a coverage threshold into
single-response labels.  RL further aims to strengthen the invariants obtained
from a single response under this inference procedure.  It applies the same
pooled filtering to groups of current responses and computes response-level
advantages from the negative-trace coverage of each response's retained
clauses and their complementary contributions across the group.  Useful
clauses thus provide training credit even when the complete response fails.  All supervision comes from open-weight model
generations, program execution, and verification; no closed model, human
labeler, or external reward model is used.

On 832 C programs, compositional inference substantially improves base-model
verification, and aligned training yields further gains.  Our primary metric,
compose@1, measures verification after filtering and composing one response.
After SFT and RL,
Qwen3-8B reaches 69.23\% compose@1, exceeding its untrained compose@32
(57.81\%) with a single response.  At the same one-response budget, it also
exceeds GPT-5 (51.32\%) and Claude Sonnet-4.6 (56.97\%) in compose@1 under
our evaluation protocol.  We use pass@1 as a standalone-response baseline.

\tightparagraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce CRAFT, aligning target-hidden compositional inference
  with supervision and reward. A shared pooled filtering procedure recovers
  useful clauses from imperfect responses, constructs SFT labels, and
  identifies the contributions rewarded by RL. With one response, base
  Qwen3-8B reaches 37.86\% compose@1 versus 6.37\% pass@1.
  \item We turn multi-response discoveries into single-response learning
  signals. SFT learns accepted compositions, raising Qwen3-8B compose@1 to
  63.94\%; RL rewards retained clauses' coverage and group contributions,
  including those from failed responses, further raising it to 69.23\%.
  \item Ablations show why aligning training with compositional inference
  matters: SFT on multi-response compositions provides more effective
  supervision than SFT on independently filtered responses, while rewarding
  retained clause contributions improves compositional verification more than
  whole-response rewards.  Whole-response success alone does not capture the
  value of generated clauses for composition.
\end{itemize}
