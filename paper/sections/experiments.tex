%% ============================================================
\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

We organize the evaluation around four questions: how much do compositional
inference and aligned training improve verification (RQ1); can CRAFT improve verification
while reducing token use relative to specialized tools (RQ2); do SFT and RL
contribute differently (RQ3); and which design choices enable these gains
(RQ4)?  Appendix~\ref{app:complete-results} collects the complete raw results;
the independent analyses are in Appendix~\ref{app:independent-experiments}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\tightparagraph{Data and judge.}
Training data comprise 9,024 RL records and 33,346 SFT records (31,865
distinct sources) of target-masked scalar-integer single-loop programs.
The raw training-program pool is drawn primarily from SV-COMP and SyGuS
\citep{svcomp,alur2013sygus}; Appendix~\ref{app:data-curation} describes
program selection and verified SFT-label construction.  SFT
labels and RL rewards are produced only by the open-weight model being trained,
program execution, and Frama-C/WP verification; no external teacher or
closed-model supervision is used.  The
evaluation combines 832 programs from Code2Inv, SyGuS, SV-COMP, LIPuS,
Clause2Inv, and Loopy: 316 linear, 50 nonlinear-arithmetic, and 466 Loopy
instances~\citep{si2020code2inv,alur2013sygus,svcomp,yu2023lipus,
cao2025clause2inv,kamath2023finding}.  No training program matches an
evaluation program under exact, alpha-renamed, or constant-abstracted matching
(Appendix~\ref{app:data-leakage}). The judge is Frama-C/WP~\citep{framac}.
Success requires it to prove induction and every restored
target; all other outcomes count as zero.

\tightparagraph{Inference protocol.}
All \sam{} evaluation runs share target masking, the canonical prompt, clause
processing, Houdini, stochastic decoding, and no re-roll or target feedback;
external tools retain their own target-hidden orchestration.  Each probe curve reuses one
saved pool of \(n_{\mathrm{probe}}\) responses per task.  Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol}
gives decoding, serving, prover, and training details.

\tightparagraph{Metrics.}
Throughout evaluation, success means \(\mathcal G\)-sufficiency under the final
verifier. Our primary metric is compose@\(k\): success under CRAFT's
filtering-and-composition pipeline on the first \(k\) saved responses. We report pass@\(k\)
as a standalone baseline: success when at least one of the same responses
independently verifies the targets. Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol}
gives the definitions.

\subsection{RQ1: How Much Do Compositional Inference and Aligned Training Improve Verification?}
\label{sec:exp:main}

Table~\ref{tab:rcf-paired} compares three local backbones across training
stages, with four API models as references. It reports the primary compose
metric and the standalone pass baseline at one- and eight-response budgets.
RL denotes training from Base;
SFT+RL starts from SFT. API models receive no additional training.

\input{sections/main_results_table}

\tightparagraph{Compositional inference alone substantially improves verification.}
For Base Qwen3-8B, compose@1 reaches 37.86\%, versus pass@1 at 6.37\%,
or \(5.94\times\) the standalone rate.
Both rates concern a one-response inference budget, showing that the framework can recover
clauses sufficient for a proof from an answer that fails as a whole.

\tightparagraph{Two-stage training aligned with inference further improves verification.}
SFT constructs supervision from composed valid clause sets, while RL assigns
credit based on the contributions of clauses retained after filtering.
Qwen3-8B compose@1 rises from 37.86\% to 63.94\% after SFT and then to
69.23\% after RL.  All three local backbones show this progressive
improvement in compose@1; Qwen3-4B SFT+RL reaches 67.55\%, exceeding
Sonnet-4.6's 56.97\% under the evaluated protocol.

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Finding.}
Compositional inference improves verification both before and after training.
Aligning SFT labels and RL rewards with this inference process further improves
verification, while trained models continue to benefit from inference-time
composition.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{RQ2: Can CRAFT Verify More While Spending Fewer Tokens?}
\label{sec:exp:tools}

We compare \sam{} with AutoSpec, SESpec, Clause2Inv, LORIS, LaM4Inv, Loopy,
and Daikon~\citep{wen2024enchanting,yang2026sespec,cao2025clause2inv,
li2026loris,wu2024lam4inv,kamath2023finding,daikon}.  LLM-based comparisons
hold GPT-5-nano, disabled reasoning, target hiding, and the final judge fixed.
Comparison protocols, supported subsets, and matched-subset results are
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:tools-comparison}.

\tightparagraph{Clause reuse improves the verification--cost frontier.}
With GPT-5-nano fixed, all three \sam{} budgets lie on the verification--token
Pareto frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:tool-pareto}). On all 832 tasks, four
responses verify \textbf{49.04\%} using \textbf{1,905 tokens per task},
exceeding all compared tools in verification rate while costing less than four of
the six LLM-based baselines.
Eight responses raise verification to 55.53\% at 2,929 tokens.
The trained model further improves the verification--token trade-off.

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Finding.}
For invariant generation with hidden verification targets and limited
informative feedback, reusable clauses improve the verification--token
trade-off. Compared with iterative repair pipelines, parallel sampling and
composition verify more programs with fewer tokens.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{RQ3: How Do SFT and RL Reshape Compositional Search?}
\label{sec:exp:rl}

Prior work suggests that RLVR can increase the sampling frequency of existing
behaviors~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}.
Figure~\ref{fig:rl-panels} compares Qwen3-8B from Base and SFT initializations
to examine whether this pattern also appears in compositional verification.

\tightparagraph{SFT expands coverage and compresses the sampling budget.}
SFT reaches 63.94\% compose@1, exceeding Base compose@32 (57.81\%).
After RL, SFT initialization raises compose@1 from 51.80\% to 69.23\%
under the same one-response budget.
It also raises compose@32 from 57.81\% to 79.93\%: SFT both makes
useful candidates accessible with fewer responses and expands verification
coverage within the measured range.

\tightparagraph{After SFT, RL mainly makes useful candidates available earlier.}
From Base, RL improves compose@1 and compose@32 by 13.94 and 12.86
points, respectively, retaining a substantial gain even at the largest budget.
After three SFT epochs, its gains shrink to \textbf{5.29 points at compose@1
and 2.05 at compose@32}. After SFT, RL mainly improves sampling
efficiency: useful candidates appear with fewer responses, while
coverage at the largest tested budget improves more modestly.

\tightparagraph{RL reveals a tension between pass and compose.}
Table~\ref{tab:rcf-paired} shows that RL's compositional gains can coincide
with lower standalone success.  For Qwen3-8B, pass@1/8 falls from
6.37\%/17.31\% to 2.88\%/7.81\% when RL starts from Base, and from
41.95\%/61.18\% to 36.78\%/56.49\% when RL starts from SFT.  The appendix
clause-cap ablation shows the same pattern: admitting more clauses improves
compose@1/8 while lowering pass@1 (Appendix~\ref{app:clause-cap}).

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Finding.}
SFT broadens verification coverage and compresses compositional search into
fewer responses.  Post-SFT RL mainly improves sampling efficiency, making
useful candidates available earlier even after substantial supervised training.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{RQ4: Why Do Composed SFT Labels and Decomposed Rewards Help?}
\label{sec:exp:ablations}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.333333\textwidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/sft_composition_compose_row.pdf}
\captionsetup{font=small,margin={0pt,7pt}}
\caption{Effect of SFT label construction on Qwen3-8B compose@\(k\).}
\label{fig:sft-composition-compose}
\end{minipage}%
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.666667\textwidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/reward_ablation_row.pdf}
\captionsetup{font=small,margin={7pt,0pt}}
\caption{Qwen3-8B reward ablation from Base: (a) compose@\(k\); (b) pass@\(k\). Vertical scales differ.}
\label{fig:reward-ablation}
\end{minipage}
\end{figure}

We ablate SFT label construction and RL credit assignment to examine how
training acquires useful clauses and preserves their contributions.

\tightparagraph{Composition turns broader exploration into supervision.}
SFT (single) uses independently filtered responses; composed labels pool
valid clauses across responses, giving supervision access to more candidate
invariants and complementary constraints.  With approximately equal numbers
of training examples, composed-label SFT raises Qwen3-8B compose@1 from
23.20\% to 63.94\% and pass@1 from 19.95\% to 41.95\%, relative to
SFT (single) (Figure~\ref{fig:sft-composition-compose}).  Composition exposes
SFT to the results of multi-response exploration, which the trained model
can exploit with a single response.

\tightparagraph{One rejected clause should not erase the others' credit.}
With verification targets hidden, RL uses inductiveness and negative-trace
coverage as its reward signal (Figure~\ref{fig:reward-ablation}).  Binary
reward reaches only 4.45\% compose@32.  Whole-rollout adds a coverage reward,
but pays it only when every clause in a response survives filtering.  Thus,
one rejected clause can withhold credit from other useful surviving clauses,
making the signal sparse.  Consistent with this, Whole-rollout reaches only
23.32\% compose@32, below Base's 57.81\%.  Clause-decomposed removes this
all-or-nothing gate and rewards retained clause contributions, reaching
66.35\% compose@32.  Relative to Base, this gain comes with a decrease in
pass@32 from 25.96\% to 21.63\%, illustrating the trade-off between
compositional verification and standalone success.

\tightparagraph{Group credit adds most from Base.}
Full adds Shapley credit with weight 0.3.  From Base, it raises compose@32
from 66.35\% to 70.67\%; after SFT, the selected Full model reaches
69.23\% compose@1 versus Clause-decomposed's 67.91\%, while compose@32 improves by
1.68 points.  Thus, the compose@32 gain from Shapley credit is 4.32 points
from Base but only 1.68 points after SFT.  This smaller marginal benefit may
indicate that SFT already enables sufficiently broad exploration of useful
clauses.  The full comparison is in Appendix~\ref{app:full-credit-analysis}.

\label{sec:exp:probe}
Appendix~\ref{app:independent-experiments} reports the complete ablations,
including inference, clause cap, filtering order,
and target visibility.

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Finding.}
Composed labels turn useful clauses from broader exploration into supervision;
clause-level rewards preserve useful contributions within failed responses.
Together, they let training exploit more of the candidates that compositional
verification can use.
\end{insightbox}
