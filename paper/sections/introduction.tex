\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Loop invariants summarize loop behavior and reduce unbounded executions to
finite proof obligations, enabling verification beyond the executions covered
by finite testing.  Their value extends beyond proving a given
postcondition: legacy code can lack explicit assertions or specifications,
yet useful invariants can capture properties for reuse in later analysis and
verification.  Even when a final postcondition is available, intervening
loops or function calls can prevent it from providing direct feedback for an
earlier loop without reasoning through the subsequent computation.  A strong
invariant, combined with the loop's exit conditions, supplies an intermediate
assertion for that later reasoning.  These settings motivate discovering
informative loop summaries from program semantics without requiring a given
verification target.

We study this problem through \emph{target-hidden loop verification}:
generation and training scores use the loop context alone, while target
assertions and postconditions \(\mathcal G\) are restored only for final
verification.  Two challenges arise.  First, without a target, proof success
cannot directly guide candidate selection or training.  Inductiveness alone
is insufficient: \(\mathsf{true}\) is a valid invariant but supplies no useful
information about loop behavior.  Ideally, an invariant should include all
reachable states while excluding as many unreachable states as possible.
However, the exact reachable set is generally difficult to obtain, making
this precision difficult to measure directly as a training reward.
We therefore need a computable, target-independent proxy for invariant
strength.  Second, an LLM response may contain both useful
and incorrect clauses, while complementary constraints may be scattered
across responses.  Rejecting or rewarding each response as a whole obscures
the value of its useful parts.  Moreover, two clauses that are not inductive
individually can form an inductive conjunction by supporting each other's
preservation.  Generation and training must therefore
account for how individual clauses contribute to a composed invariant.

We introduce \sam{} to align compositional inference, supervision labels,
and RL rewards.  At inference, it decomposes responses into clauses, pools
them for joint verification, repeatedly removes unproved clauses, and
conjoins the survivors.  During training, the fraction of execution-derived
candidate negative traces rejected by verified clauses supplies a
target-independent strength proxy.  SFT uses verified multi-response
compositions that meet a coverage threshold as single-response training
labels; RL rewards the coverage of clauses
retained by the same filtering procedure, with Shapley credit allocating
shared coverage among contributing responses.  Useful parts can thus receive
credit even when the complete response fails.  The two stages aim to make
multi-response discoveries accessible with fewer samples.  All supervision
comes from the model's own generations, program execution, and verification,
without an external teacher or reward model.

Evaluation on 832 C programs shows that compositional inference substantially
improves bare-model verification and that aligned training yields further
gains.  We use compose@1, the verification rate after filtering one response,
as our primary metric.

\tightparagraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce a compositional inference framework for target-hidden
  invariant generation.  Pooling clauses before Houdini filtering preserves
  useful parts of imperfect responses and combines complementary constraints.
  With one response, bare Qwen3-8B reaches 37.86\% compose@1 versus
  6.37\% pass@1.
  \item We align SFT labels and RL credit with compositional inference.
  SFT distills accepted multi-response compositions into single-response
  supervision, raising Qwen3-8B compose@1 to 63.94\%.  RL rewards surviving
  clauses' coverage and group contributions even when the complete response
  fails, further raising compose@1 to 69.23\%.
  \item Our experiments reveal that whole-response success and compositional
  verification can diverge.  SFT broadens verification coverage and compresses
  the sampling budget; after substantial SFT, RL mainly improves sampling
  efficiency at small budgets, with smaller coverage gains at the largest
  tested budget.
\end{itemize}
\end{samepage}
