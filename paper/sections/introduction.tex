\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Loop invariants turn unbounded executions into finite proofs, but validity
alone does not imply usefulness: \(\mathit{true}\) is valid for every loop and
proves nothing downstream.  At the same time, valid invariants are closed
under conjunction.  A range bound from one response and a relational equality
from another can therefore prove a target that neither response proves alone,
while clause-wise verification can discard failures without losing useful
clauses.  This makes the filtered cross-response clause pool, rather than one
complete response, the natural inference object.

Whole-response rewards are mismatched to this object.  They erase credit for
a useful clause when a sibling clause fails, and they value a complementary
constraint no more than one repeated by every peer.  More generally,
response-level RLVR can sharpen probability on already-supported answers
without expanding large-\(k\) coverage~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}, potentially
reducing the diversity that composition needs.  Our central claim is therefore
an alignment claim: the training reward should value the same verified
composition that inference constructs from a fixed rollout group, rather than
each rollout as an independent final answer.

We study this claim under \emph{target-hidden loop verification}.  The model
sees the loop context but not the target assertions and postconditions \(\mathcal G\).
After the invariant set is fixed, the untouched source is restored and the
verifier checks both induction and every original target.  Hiding \(\mathcal G\)
prevents target copying and target-specific proof feedback, so generation and
training require a graded, target-independent strength signal.

At inference, \sam{} implements
\emph{rollout--decompose--pool--filter--compose}: given \(k\) responses, it
extracts and pools clauses, filters them with syntax checks and Houdini, and
then restores \(\mathcal G\).  compose@\(k\) measures the verified rate of this composed
prefix, preserving partial answers that pass@\(k\) discards.  Training is
aligned to this object at the same two compositional levels.  Within a rollout,
execution-derived negative coverage credits the clauses retained by the
inference filter; across each GRPO group~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}, a
closed-form Shapley term credits coverage not already supplied by peers.  SFT
distills the same filtered multi-response union into one response.

\tightparagraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We formulate target-hidden verification around fixed-budget filtered
  composition and introduce compose@\(k\) to evaluate it.
  \item We align training with the two compositional levels used at inference:
  clause-level negative coverage rewards what survives within a response,
  group-level Shapley allocation rewards what complements other responses, and
  SFT distills the same filtered union, all without exposing \(\mathcal G\).
  \item On 832 C programs, four target-hidden GPT-5-nano responses
  verify 49.0\% of tasks, versus 42.2\% for the strongest external baseline
  under system-native fixed budgets.  Controlled probes and ablations
  separate standalone success, composition, and the two levels of credit.
\end{itemize}
\end{samepage}
