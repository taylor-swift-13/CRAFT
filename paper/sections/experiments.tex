%% ============================================================
\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

We ask how combination scales with sampling (RQ1), transfers across models
(RQ2), compares with specialized tools (RQ3), changes after RL (RQ4), and
depends on reward and sampler design (RQ5).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\paragraph{Data and judge.}
The cleaned training artifacts contain 37,481 target-masked scalar-integer
single-loop RL records; 3,200 form SFT targets.  Evaluation uses the standard
benchmark suites of prior learning-based invariant-generation systems: 832~C
programs, comprising 316 linear programs (133 \textsc{Code2Inv}
\citep{si2020code2inv}, 84 SyGuS 2019 \citep{alur2013sygus}, 99 SV-COMP 2024
\citep{svcomp}) and 50 nonlinear-arithmetic (NLA) programs (30 LIPuS
\citep{yu2023lipus}, 20 SV-COMP 2024) as released with
\textsc{Clause2Inv}~\citep{cao2025clause2inv}, plus 466 integer programs from
Loopy~\citep{kamath2023finding}.  The overlap and prompt-sanitation audits
are in Appendix~\ref{app:data-leakage}.  We remove non-contract comments and
mask all target predicates before generation, then restore the untouched
source only after invariants are fixed.
Success requires Frama-C/WP~\citep{framac}, under a 5-second per-obligation
prover budget, to prove establishment, preservation, and every restored
target; all failures count as zero.

\paragraph{Inference protocol.}
All \sam{} evaluation runs share target masking, the canonical prompt, clause
processing, and Houdini; external tools retain their own target-hidden orchestration.  Within
\sam{}, API models use \texttt{reasoning\_effort=none}; all locally served
checkpoints use non-thinking decoding to match data synthesis and training.  Decoding uses
temperature 1.0 and top-\(p\) 1.0.  API calls cap completions at 8,192 tokens,
including provider-internal reasoning tokens; local vLLM calls cap each
response at 2,048 emitted tokens.  We use no re-roll or target feedback.  RQ1
reuses one saved batch of 100 stochastic responses per task.
Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol} gives benchmark provenance, serving,
and training details.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Throughout evaluation, success means \(Q\)-sufficiency under the final
verifier.  pass@\(k\) is the probability that at least one of \(k\) responses
proves \(Q\).  Whenever a saved pool contains \(c\) successful responses among
\(n=100\), we use the unbiased estimator
\(\widehat{\operatorname{pass@}k}=1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\) for that
program and average it over programs.
Only responses that prove \(Q\) count as successes.  combine@\(k\) is the
fraction of programs proved by unioning the first \(k\) responses and applying
Houdini.  pass@\(k\) requires one response to contain the whole proof, whereas
combine@\(k\) can combine clauses from different responses.  We do not compute
the exact all-subset analogue for combine@\(k\), because it would require
filtering \(\binom{100}{k}\) unions per program; we evaluate the fixed saved
prefix instead.  For the measured budget grid
\(\mathcal K=\{1,10,30,50,100\}\), we report
\(k_{95}=\min\{k\in\mathcal K:
\operatorname{Comb}_\pi(k)\geq0.95\,\operatorname{Comb}_\pi(100)\}\), where
\(\operatorname{Comb}_\pi(k)\) is the measured combine@\(k\) curve.  A smaller
\(k_{95}\) means the retained pool reaches its large-budget performance
sooner.

\subsection{RQ1: How Do pass@k and combine@k Scale?}
\label{sec:exp:probe}

We sample 100 target-hidden responses per task from six base checkpoints:
Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-30B-A3B
~\citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Llama 3.1 8B.  Complete values and \(k_{95}\) appear in
Appendix~\ref{tab:probe-complete}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{figures/base_model_probe.pdf}
\caption{pass@\(k\) (unbiased estimate) and prefix combine@\(k\).
Combination saturates by \(k=30\), while pass@\(k\) keeps growing.}
\label{fig:base-model-probe}
\end{figure}

At \(k=1\), prefix combine is 5.91--39.23 points above the unbiased pass
estimate across checkpoints.  Because these are different estimands rather
than paired first-response outcomes, we treat this gap as descriptive; RQ2
reports the paired effect of processing the same response.  In the retained
response pools, every model
reaches 95\% of combine@100 by \(k=30\), with at most 1.68 additional points
through \(k=100\).  In contrast, pass@30 reaches only 58.1--85.0\% of
pass@100 on the five Qwen checkpoints and gains another 4.41--6.60 points by
\(k=100\).  For Qwen3-0.6B, 102/832 programs have at least one successful
response, giving pass@100 of 12.26\%; combine rises from 55/832 (6.61\%) at
\(k=1\) to 208/832 (25.00\%) at \(k=30\), with no further gain through
\(k=100\).  Llama 3.1 8B is harder in standalone proof generation---pass@100
is 7.33\%---but shows the same compositional pattern: combine rises from
16.59\% at \(k=1\) to 34.13\% at \(k=30\), then changes by only 0.25 points.
Combination thus
front-loads useful coverage, whereas standalone proofs require wider sampling.
The combine@100 ceilings (25.00--58.05\%) further suggest that these pools
remain bottlenecked by clause quality or diversity: extending the budget to
100 responses does not remove the gap.

\begin{tcolorbox}[
  colback=algobg,
  colframe=algoframe,
  boxrule=0.6pt,
  arc=2pt,
  left=5pt,right=5pt,top=4pt,bottom=4pt
]
\textbf{Insight.} pass@1 is not an appropriate primary metric for
compositional invariant generation.  By treating a response as indivisible,
it assigns zero both to useless outputs and to incomplete outputs containing
valid, reusable clauses.  It remains a standalone diagnostic, whereas
combine@\(k\) directly measures whether clauses found within the deployment
budget form a sufficient invariant after filtering.
\end{tcolorbox}

\paragraph{Failure modes under target hiding.}
The specialized systems degrade for different structural reasons.  In the
evaluated one-round configuration, \textsc{AutoSpec} requests eight
completions in parallel, pools their clauses, and verifier-filters the union;
it is therefore already an eight-proposal combination method rather than a
single-proposal baseline.  Nevertheless, it verifies 351 programs, 23 fewer
than \sam{} combine@1, while using 2,181.72 versus 1,135.83 tokens per task.
Its extra samples mainly add redundant or weak clauses rather than the
complementary facts recovered by \sam{}'s clause processing.  \textsc{SESpec}
instead makes 5.09 sequential calls per completed task on average.  This
refinement-heavy schedule spends 22,081.61 tokens but does not provide the
independent parallel exploration needed when the target is absent; moreover,
errors from the target-hidden verifier diagnose syntax and inductiveness, not
which missing relation would establish the hidden goal.  \textsc{Clause2Inv}'s
ICE loop normally uses false-state (\texttt{F:}) counterexamples induced by the
postcondition.  Target-hidden execution suppresses the postcondition check and
retains only pre-state and transition counterexamples, removing its principal
goal-directed feedback signal; its required transition VCs are also
unavailable for all 466 Loopy programs.

\textsc{Loopy} exhibits a separate interface failure.  Its extractor accepts
only the first valid Markdown-fenced code block.  Although 98.8\% of the
non-reasoning initial responses contain textual \texttt{loop invariant}
clauses, only 1.75\% use the required fence and reach Houdini; for repair
responses the corresponding rates are 94.5\% and 1.13\%.  Houdini can prune
bad clauses but cannot recover discarded ones.  Target hiding further removes
the assertion-specific checker diagnostic expected by Loopy's repair prompt,
leaving only generic inductiveness feedback: repair adds just 15 successes to
the 141 initially solved tasks, for 156/832 (18.75\%) overall.  Thus this row
primarily measures a brittle parser and a repair protocol mismatched to the
hidden-target setting, rather than the quality of the invariants present in
the raw responses.  The archived medium-reasoning run is reported separately
in the appendix.

\label{sec:exp:rl}
