%% ============================================================
\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

We ask how composition scales with sampling (RQ1), how much \sam{} improves
compose@$k$ over standalone responses (RQ2), how \sam{} compares with specialized tools (RQ3),
how much RL adds (RQ4), and
what drives the training gain (RQ5).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\tightparagraph{Data and judge.}
Training data comprise 4,992 RL records and 33,346 SFT records (31,865
distinct sources) of target-masked scalar-integer single-loop programs;
Appendix~\ref{app:data-curation} gives their synthesis and verification.  SFT
targets and RL rewards are produced only by the open-weight model being trained,
program execution, and Frama-C/WP verification; no external teacher or
closed-model supervision is used.  The
evaluation combines 832 programs from Code2Inv, SyGuS, SV-COMP, LIPuS,
Clause2Inv, and Loopy: 316 linear, 50 nonlinear-arithmetic, and 466 Loopy
instances~\citep{si2020code2inv,alur2013sygus,svcomp,yu2023lipus,
cao2025clause2inv,kamath2023finding}.  No training program matches an
evaluation program under exact, alpha-renamed, or constant-abstracted matching
(Appendix~\ref{app:data-leakage}).  The judge is Frama-C 27.1 (Cobalt) with
the WP plug-in, Typed memory model, and Alt-Ergo/Z3 prover portfolio
~\citep{framac}.  Success requires it to prove induction and every restored
target; all other outcomes count as zero.

\tightparagraph{Inference protocol.}
All \sam{} evaluation runs share target masking, the canonical prompt, clause
processing, Houdini, stochastic decoding, and no re-roll or target feedback;
external tools retain their own target-hidden orchestration.  RQ1 reuses one
saved pool of \(n_{\mathrm{probe}}\) responses per task.  Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol}
gives decoding, serving, prover, and training details.

\tightparagraph{Metrics.}
Throughout evaluation, success means \(\mathcal G\)-sufficiency under the final
verifier.  pass@\(k\) estimates whether any of \(k\) responses succeeds alone;
compose@\(k\) measures whether filtering their clause union succeeds.  The
former uses the standard unbiased estimator, while the latter evaluates saved
response prefixes; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol} gives both
estimators.  We also report \(k_{\alpha}\), the smallest \(k\in\mathcal K\)
whose compose@\(k\) reaches the saturation fraction \(\alpha\) of
compose@\(k_{\max}\), where \(k_{\max}=\max\mathcal K\).  A smaller
\(k_{\alpha}\) means the retained pool reaches its large-budget performance sooner.

\subsection{RQ1: How Do pass@k and compose@k Scale?}
\label{sec:exp:probe}

We sample \(n_{\mathrm{probe}}\) target-hidden responses per task from six base checkpoints:
Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-30B-A3B
~\citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Llama 3.1 8B.  Complete curves, \(k_{\alpha}\), and
whole-workload values appear in Appendix~\ref{tab:probe-complete}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{figures/base_model_probe.pdf}
\caption{pass@\(k\) (unbiased estimate) and prefix compose@\(k\).
Composition saturates by \(k{\approx}8\)--\(16\), while pass@\(k\) keeps growing.}
\label{fig:base-model-probe}
\end{figure}

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight.}
Standalone completion and filtered composition scale differently.  Across
all six checkpoints, compose@16 is within 2.6 points of compose@32, whereas
pass@\(k\) continues improving through \(k{=}32\).  Llama 3.1 8B shows the
same pattern despite low standalone accuracy (pass@32 9.8\%): composition
rises from 27.9\% at \(k{=}1\) to 51.7\% at \(k{=}16\).  Thus the first dozen
samples contain most reusable clauses, while complete standalone proofs
benefit from wider sampling.  Performance is not monotone in nominal model
size: Qwen3-30B-A3B reaches 43.87\% compose@32, below the 8B and 14B dense
checkpoints.  RQ2 gives the matched pipeline comparison.
\end{insightbox}

62.62--66.35\% compose@1 and

budgets.

\subsection{RQ4: How Much Does Reinforcement Learning Improve Inference?}
\label{sec:exp:rl}

We compare matched Qwen3-8B checkpoints before and after RL, holding inference
fixed within each pair.

\tightparagraph{What to expect.}
Whole-response RL can sharpen probability mass around existing
answers~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}; our clause-level group reward instead
predicts higher compose@\(k\), especially at small budgets, without requiring
higher standalone accuracy.

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight.}
RL raises Qwen3-8B compose@1 from 37.86\% to 57.93\%, a 20.07-point absolute
and 53.0\% relative gain over the untrained pipeline, while preserving pass@1
(6.80\% vs.\ 6.43\%).  Relative to bare pass@1, the RL path gains 51.50
points (800.9\%) and exceeds the untrained Qwen3-14B pipeline by 14.42 points.
One RL sample
already matches the initialization's compose@32 (57.81\%), a 32-fold budget
reduction, and compose@32 rises to 73.20\%.  This is the predicted
redistribution toward complementary clauses rather than better whole
responses.  Qwen3-14B shows the same compositional shift
(Appendix~\ref{tab:rl-complete}), although its pass@1 also rises.
\end{insightbox}

\centering

\subsection{RQ5: What Drives the Training Gain?}
\label{sec:exp:ablations}

We ablate the reward and negative sampler on Qwen3-8B from the Zero
initialization, changing only the component under study.  Exact definitions
and numbers are in Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-definitions}.

\tightparagraph{Reward-function ablation.}
Binary and Whole-rollout give response-level credit; Clause-decomposed pays
each response according to the coverage of its surviving subset \(V_i\), and
Full adds the group credit \(w_g\phi_i\)
from Equation~\eqref{eq:generation-reward}.  All variants process at most
\(m_{\max}\) clauses per response.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/reward_ablation.pdf}
\caption{Reward-function ablation on Qwen3-8B (Zero initialization).
  Whole-rollout credit raises pass@1 (b) but collapses compose@\(k\) (a)
  far below the untrained model; binary credit collapses both.
  Clause-decomposed credit surpasses the untrained compose curve, and Full
  is best at every budget.
  Dotted: Zero, the untrained checkpoint.  Exact numbers in
  Table~\ref{tab:reward-ablation} (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-definitions}).}
\label{fig:reward-ablation}
\end{figure}

\begin{insightbox}
\textbf{Insight: credit structure controls exploration.}
Binary and whole-response RLVR treat each rollout as indivisible and fail on
the compositional objective.  Whole-rollout credit raises pass@1 by 10.24
points, sharpening probability around complete answers, but reduces
compose@32 by 35.09 points (60.7\% relative); binary credit lowers both
metrics.  Clause-decomposed credit preserves useful parts of partially
correct responses and improves compose@1 and compose@32 over the untrained
model by 10.22 and 4.09 points.  Group credit then discounts repeated
coverage and adds another 9.85--11.30 points, bringing the full reward's gains
to 20.07 points at compose@1 and 15.39 points at compose@32.  Matching the
credit unit to decompose--compose inference provides denser feedback and
mitigates the exploration collapse of whole-response GRPO.
\end{insightbox}

\tightparagraph{Negative-sampler ablation.}
Under the full reward, the structured sampler (relation and post-exit
families, Section~\ref{sec:method:sampler}) exceeds uniform random
perturbations by 2.0--5.3 compose@\(k\) points, with little pass@\(k\) change
(Table~\ref{tab:sampler-ablation}).

\tightparagraph{Does negative coverage predict hidden-target utility?}
Across 5,606 continuously scored target-hidden candidate sets, negative
coverage predicts restored-target success with AUROC \(0.738\) (clustered
95\% CI \(0.711\)--\(0.763\)) and AUPRC \(0.730\).  Within-program ranking is
stronger (macro AUROC \(0.794\), permutation \(p<2\times10^{-4}\)).  The
association weakens on NLA, so coverage is useful but not universal;
Appendix~\ref{app:coverage-predictiveness} gives the full protocol.
