\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

Verifying any program with a loop requires finding an adequate invariant: no
proof can otherwise cover arbitrarily many iterations, and writing invariants
by hand remains a central bottleneck to verifying real software.  Loop
invariants summarize an unbounded computation in a finite assertion.  They
must be inductive while retaining enough program knowledge to establish
the desired property at loop exit.  A verifier gives a sharp test of
soundness, but not a graded measure of precision: \(\mathit{true}\) can be
inductive while proving nothing useful.  Moreover, the exact reachable-state
set may be expensive to compute or inexpressible in the annotation language.
The learning problem is therefore not merely to produce a sound invariant,
but to approach a stronger sound over-approximation.  We formalize soundness,
logical strength, and target sufficiency in Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}.

We study this alignment problem under \emph{target-hidden loop verification},
where the target is withheld until after the invariant set has been fixed
(Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}).  Existing methods can use the target, failed
obligations, or counterexamples to guide search
~\citep{gulwani2008program,gupta2009invgen,dillig2013inductive,
alur2013sygus,garg2014ice,garg2016learning,si2020code2inv,
kamath2023finding,cao2025clause2inv}.  Hiding \(Q\) prevents copying an
inductive postcondition.  Final success is unchanged: the verifier must prove
soundness and sufficiency for the restored target.  This rules out direct
target sufficiency as a training reward and makes a dense, target-independent
strength signal necessary.

The same task also offers a useful structure: sound invariants compose.  The
conjunction of two inductive predicates is inductive and no weaker than either
predicate.  One response may provide a bound and another a relational
equality, so their union can prove a target that neither proves alone.  We
therefore treat the filtered composition of a fixed rollout group as the
deployed answer.  Our objective is combine@\(k\), rather than pass@1 or
pass@\(k\): with as few responses as possible, the policy should discover a
clause pool whose union survives Houdini and proves the restored target.  This
compositionality is the organizing principle of our method: every other
component is designed to make rollout--combine--filter effective under a
finite sampling budget.

This objective exposes a training--inference mismatch.  An independent
verifier reward assigns the same score to a rollout regardless of what its
peers already discovered.  It cannot distinguish a new useful relation from a
redundant copy, and can collapse repeated sampling onto the same easy
high-reward clauses.  A whole-response pass/fail reward also discards partial
progress inside an imperfect response: one invalid clause can obscure several
useful ones.

We address credit assignment along both axes.  Within each rollout, we split
conjunctions into clauses and use the verifier as a projection operator: syntax
filtering and Houdini retain a sound subset, but provide no extra reward merely
because the original response is syntactically valid or every proposed clause
survives.  We reward the behavioral strength of the retained subset, so useful
clauses receive partial credit even when their response is imperfect.  Across
rollouts, a closed-form Shapley allocation credits each subset for negative
coverage not already supplied by its peers.  The resulting reward is therefore
both clause-decomposed within a response and group-conditioned across
responses.

\sam{} implements this idea with one rollout--combine--filter operator shared
by training and deployment (Section~\ref{sec:method}).  SFT distills a filtered
multi-response union into one stronger response.  GRPO supplies groups of
rollouts, and our group-conditioned reward trains each response for its
contribution to their composition.  At inference, the same clause splitting,
union, syntax filtering, and Houdini pruning produce the final invariant before
\(Q\) is restored.

Our experiments deliberately separate standalone success from combined
inference.  On 832 integer C programs, five target-hidden responses with union
and Houdini verify 622 programs (74.76\%), compared with 514 (61.78\%) for the
strongest external baseline.  On Qwen3-8B, reinforcement learning decreases
direct pass@1 from 9.30\% to 4.88\% but increases combine@10 from 52.40\% to
56.97\%.  Ten responses from the trained policy exceed combine@100 of the base
model, reducing the rollouts needed to reach 95\% of the measured maximum from
30 to 10.  Training thus compresses broad base-model exploration into a much
smaller deployment budget rather than optimizing one standalone
target-proving answer.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We formulate target-hidden loop verification around
  rollout--combine--filter: the deployed and optimized object is the verified
  composition of a fixed response group, measured by combine@\(k\), rather
  than a standalone response measured by pass@1.
  \item We align training credit with that compositional object.  Synthetic
  SFT targets distill filtered combinations, while RL decomposes credit over
  the sound clauses within each rollout and composes credit across rollouts
  according to their complementary contribution, without observing \(Q\).
  \item We show that this alignment improves both combined verification and
  rollout efficiency, and audit the synthetic negative examples that support
  the reward.
\end{itemize}
