%% ============================================================
\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

We organize the evaluation around five questions.  RQ1 probes how direct
pass@\(k\) and combined inference scale with the sampling budget.  RQ2 compares
pass@1 and combine@1 across model families.  RQ3 compares the resulting system
with specialized invariant-generation tools.  RQ4 isolates the improvement
from RL under both zero-shot and SFT initialization.  RQ5 ablates the reward
terms and negative-example sampler.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\paragraph{Data sources and benchmarks.}
Training uses a separate pool of 37,958 target-hidden, scalar-integer,
single-loop program records drawn primarily from SV-COMP and SyGuS; the 3,454
programs used to construct SFT targets are a subset of this pool.
Appendix~\ref{app:data-leakage} audits overlap between this training pool and
evaluation.  We evaluate 832 integer C programs.  From the \textsc{Clause2Inv}
release, we use 316 linear programs (133 from \textsc{Code2Inv}, 84 from SyGuS
2019, and 99 from SV-COMP 2024) and 50 nonlinear programs (30 from LIPuS and 20
from SV-COMP 2024)~\citep{yu2023lipus,cao2025clause2inv}.  The remaining
466 programs are the integer fragment of Loopy's
original 469-program corpus; three floating-point programs are excluded
because they fall outside our scalar-integer predicate language.  Unsupported
inputs remain failures in the common denominator.  Because these collections
reuse benchmarks from earlier work, 832 denotes the common file-level
evaluation workload rather than 832 independently sourced problems.

\paragraph{Target-hidden evaluation.}
Before any model-facing step, we remove \texttt{assert} and
\texttt{ensures} predicates, executable assertion calls, and conventional
error-label names.  Preconditions and executable loop semantics remain
visible.  After a method fixes its invariants, they are inserted into the
untouched original program.  A task succeeds only if the source parses,
Frama-C/WP proves establishment and preservation for every retained
invariant, and every restored target goal succeeds.  Failures, timeouts,
malformed outputs, and unsupported programs count as zero.

\paragraph{Verifier and inference.}
All final checks use Frama-C 31.0 (Gallium)~\citep{framac}, WP, and Z3
4.13.3.  Neural experiments use the same target-hiding code, generation
template, clause splitter, semantic deduplication, and Houdini implementation.
We use each model's serving interface and chat template.  API models use the
service's default reasoning configuration; we do not override their reasoning
effort at inference time.  For checkpoints participating in our SFT or RL
training pipeline, explicit thinking is disabled during evaluation to match
the synthetic-data and training-rollout configuration.  Unless an RQ varies
\(k\), generation
uses temperature 1.0, top-\(p\) 1.0, at most 2,048 new tokens, and no re-roll
or target-conditioned feedback.  We do not set an additional top-\(k\) cutoff
or repetition penalty beyond the serving-interface defaults.  For the
\(k\)-sweep, one
batched request draws 100 responses per program, and every pass@\(k\) and
combine@\(k\) point reuses that same saved response pool.  We do not fix a
per-response sampling seed; the 100 outputs are stochastic draws from that
single batch.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Pass@\(k\) is the probability that at least one of \(k\) responses proves
\(Q\).  Given \(c\) successful responses among \(n=100\), we estimate it as
\(\widehat{\operatorname{pass@}k}=1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\).
Only responses that prove \(Q\) count as successes.  Combine@\(k\) is the
fraction of programs proved by unioning the first \(k\) responses and applying
Houdini.  Pass@\(k\) requires one response to contain the whole proof, whereas
combine@\(k\) can combine clauses from different responses.  We also report
\(k_{95}=\min\{k:C_\pi(k)\geq0.95\,C_\pi(100)\}\), the smallest rollout
budget that reaches 95\% of combine@100.  A smaller \(k_{95}\) means higher
sampling efficiency.

\paragraph{Pending-result convention.}
Unfinished result cells below are marked \emph{TBD} and remain empty until the
corresponding common-protocol run is complete; populated cells come only from
completed runs, and we do not extrapolate missing values from pilots.

\subsection{RQ1: How Do pass@k and combine@k Scale?}
\label{sec:exp:probe}

We sample 100 target-hidden responses per task from four base checkpoints:
Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-30B-A3B
~\citep{yang2025qwen3}.  All points for one checkpoint are computed from the
same saved response pool.  We evaluate every recorded prefix; the complete
numeric results, including \(k_{95}\), appear in
Appendix~\ref{tab:probe-complete}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/qwen_official_probe.pdf}
\caption{Scaling of pass@\(k\) and combine@\(k\), shown together in a
separate panel for each base checkpoint.  Pass@\(k\) uses the unbiased
estimator, whereas combine@\(k\) evaluates the saved response prefix;
markers denote measured budgets rather than interpolated evaluations.
Combination provides a large early gain and is nearly saturated by
\(k=30\), while pass@\(k\) continues to grow at larger budgets.}
\label{fig:base-model-probe}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Combination is effective and saturates early.}
Already at \(k=1\), combine@1 exceeds pass@1 by 31.36, 15.58, 39.23, and
35.84 percentage points for the 4B, 8B, 14B, and 30B-A3B checkpoints,
respectively.  This large gap shows that splitting, filtering, and recombining
clauses recovers proofs even when the original response is not independently
\(Q\)-sufficient.  Yet almost all of this benefit is obtained quickly: every
checkpoint reaches at least 95\% of its combine@100 value by \(k=30\), and
the largest additional gain from \(k=30\) to \(k=100\) is only 0.60 points.
Thus, approximately 30 responses capture the practical combination ceiling
in this probe.

\paragraph{Direct pass converges more slowly.}
At \(k=30\), pass@\(k\) has reached only 71.8--85.0\% of its pass@100 value
across the four checkpoints.  Increasing the budget from 30 to 100 still
adds 4.41--6.60 percentage points, in clear contrast to the nearly flat
combine@\(k\) curves.  Combination therefore front-loads useful proof
coverage, whereas finding a single complete response continues to benefit
from substantially wider sampling.

The small non-monotonicity in the 8B combine curve reflects the deployed
metric: finite Frama-C/WP timeouts count as failures, and a larger clause pool
can increase proof cost.  We therefore interpret combine@\(k\) as an
operational success curve rather than assuming mathematical monotonicity.

As a reasoning-control diagnostic, we also ran GPT-5 with
\texttt{reasoning\_effort=none}.  Under that setting, pass@1 is
251/832 (30.17\%) and combine@1 is 477/832 (57.33\%), at 1,028.12 mean
tokens per task.  We retain this run as an ablation rather than mixing it into
the provider-default main row.

Combine@1 isolates a useful property of the framework: even with one model
call, a response need not be accepted or rejected as a monolithic object.
Houdini can retain its sound clauses while removing a failing clause.  The
comparison tests whether this lightweight inference step gives particularly
large gains to open models, whose raw responses may be less reliable.

\paragraph{Efficiency and orchestration.}
\sam{} does not maintain a multi-turn repair conversation, workflow state, or
growing context history.  Its orchestration state is only a set of independent
clauses: generate, split, normalize, union, and filter.  We report generated
tokens, verifier invocations, wall time, and peak prompt length so
that effectiveness is not separated from workflow cost.  This RQ tests whether
the simple framework is competitive with specialized systems without their
context management, and whether its gains remain substantial on open models.
With the same \texttt{gpt-5-nano} backbone, \sam{} improves the naive baseline
from 45.55\% to 54.33\% at pass@1 and to 60.58\% at combine@1.

\subsection{RQ4: How Much Does Reinforcement Learning Improve Inference?}
\label{sec:exp:rl}

We use Qwen3-8B and separate two paired training paths.  The zero-shot path
compares the base checkpoint with RL-Zero.  The supervised path compares the
SFT checkpoint immediately before RL with the checkpoint obtained by
continuing from it under the same RL objective.  Each after-RL model therefore
has a matched before-RL initialization, preventing SFT gains from being

increase standalone proofs.

\subsection{RQ5: What Drives the Training Gain?}
\label{sec:exp:ablations}

Both ablation studies use Qwen3-8B and hold prompts, data order, rollout-group
size, update budget, seeds, and inference code fixed.

\paragraph{Reward-function ablation.}
We separate the two axes of our credit assignment from the density of the
strength signal.  The conventional baseline assigns a binary verifier reward
to a complete response.  The second row replaces this binary outcome with
negative coverage, but remains all-or-nothing: it receives coverage only when
every admitted clause survives the positive-example and Houdini filters,
\[
  r_i^{\mathrm{whole}}
  =\mathbf 1[S_i=\bar A_i]\,\frac{|R_i|}{|\mathcal N|}
  -w_o o(A_i).
\]
The third row computes the same coverage on the surviving clause subset
\(S_i\), isolating \emph{intra-rollout decomposition}.  The fourth row adds
Shapley allocation across \(R_{1:G}\), isolating \emph{inter-rollout
composition}.  The final row adds only the duplicate regularizer.  The
response cap and overflow cost are held fixed across all
four coverage-based rows.  Thus each adjacent comparison introduces one
design decision.
The rows select \texttt{reward\_variant}=\texttt{binary},
\texttt{whole\_coverage}, \texttt{base}, \texttt{base\_shapley}, and
\texttt{full}, respectively.  We call these variants \emph{Binary
Verification}, \emph{Whole-Rollout Strength}, \emph{Clause-Decomposed
Strength}, \emph{Compositional Credit}, and \emph{Regularized Compositional
Credit}.

\paragraph{Negative-sampling ablation.}
We compare only the two externally exposed samplers.  Both use the same
executions, reachable bases, safety filters, seed, and 512-trace total cap.
\emph{Random} perturbs one or two variables with uniformly sampled local
offsets.  \emph{Structured} combines relational perturbations, post-exit
continuations, and range violations under fixed family budgets.  This
cap-matched comparison tests whether loop-specific structure matters beyond
the number of sampled negatives.  The rows select
\texttt{negative\_sampler}=\texttt{random} and \texttt{structured}.

Besides final verification, we report reward variance and hard-collision
audits.  Reward variance tests whether a sampler supplies enough
discrimination for group-relative RL.  The collision audit checks whether a
generated negative is also observed as reachable under expanded execution
sampling, guarding against gains obtained from systematically incorrect
negative labels.
