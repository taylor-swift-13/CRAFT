%% ============================================================
\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
%% ============================================================

We ask how composition scales with sampling (RQ1), how much each pipeline
component contributes (RQ2), how \sam{} compares with specialized tools (RQ3),
how much RL adds (RQ4), and
what drives the training gain (RQ5).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\paragraph{Data and judge.}
Training uses 4,992 RL and 33,346 SFT records from 31,865 target-masked
single-loop programs; SFT targets are generated with GPT-5.6 Luna and
verified by Frama-C/WP (Appendix~\ref{app:data-curation}).  Evaluation covers
832 C programs: 316 linear and 50 NLA tasks from Code2Inv, SyGuS, SV-COMP,
and LIPuS, plus 466 Loopy tasks
\citep{si2020code2inv,alur2013sygus,svcomp,yu2023lipus,kamath2023finding}.
No train--test match remains under exact, alpha-renamed, or
constant-abstracted comparison (Appendix~\ref{app:data-leakage}).  Success
requires Frama-C/WP to prove induction and every restored target with 5 s per
obligation; all other outcomes are failures.

\paragraph{Inference protocol.}
CRAFT runs share masking, prompting, clause processing, and Houdini; external
tools retain their native target-hidden orchestration.  Decoding uses
temperature 1, top-\(p=1\), no re-roll, and no target feedback.  API models
disable reasoning and cap completions at 8,192 tokens; local checkpoints use
non-thinking decoding and 2,048 emitted tokens.  RQ1 reuses one 32-response
pool per local checkpoint and task.  Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-protocol}
fixes all serving and training settings.

\paragraph{Provisional presentation values.}
\mock{Red values and \(\dagger\) rows are internally consistent mock results,
not empirical findings; all must be replaced before submission.}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
pass@\(k\) measures whether any response proves \(Q\), using the unbiased
estimator \(1-\binom{n-s}{k}/\binom nk\) from \(s\) successes in \(n\) saved
responses.  compose@\(k\) instead unions the first \(k\) responses, filters
them, and reports the verified-program fraction; it is a fixed-prefix metric,
not an average over subsets.  We also report \(k_{95}\), the smallest
\(k\in\{1,4,8,16,32\}\) reaching 95\% of compose@32.

\subsection{RQ1: How Do pass@\(k\) and compose@\(k\) Scale?}
\label{sec:exp:probe}

We sample 32 target-hidden responses per task from six base checkpoints:
Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-30B-A3B
~\citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Llama 3.1 8B.  Complete curves, \(k_{95}\), and
per-stratum breakdowns appear in Table~\ref{tab:probe-complete}.

At \(k{=}1\), compose@1 exceeds the unbiased pass@1 estimate by
14.9--42.4 points across checkpoints.
In the retained response pools, compose@16 is within 2.1 points of
compose@32 on every checkpoint (\(k_{95}\in\{8,16,32\}\);
Table~\ref{tab:probe-complete}).  In contrast, pass@8 reaches only
72--74\% of pass@32 on the five Qwen checkpoints and gains another
5.0--7.8 points by \(k{=}32\).  Llama 3.1 8B is harder in standalone proof
generation---pass@32 is 13.8\%---but shows the same compositional pattern:
compose rises from 29.2\% at \(k{=}1\) to 56.0\% at \(k{=}16\), then
changes by only 0.1 points.

\paragraph{Insight.}
Composition finds most of its useful
clauses within the first dozen samples, whereas standalone proofs require
wider sampling.  The compose@32 values (41.4--67.8\%) further suggest that
these pools remain limited by clause quality or diversity: extending the
budget to 32 responses does not remove the gap.

rows judge each response as one indivisible conjunction (pass@\(k\));
pipeline rows decompose the same responses into clauses, filter them, and

checkpoint.  The three Qwen3 models add the
training stages (+SFT, +SFT+RL) and two ablation rows that repeat the
trained checkpoints without the pipeline; Llama 3.1 8B reports the
SFT rows without RL.

\subsection{RQ3: How Does \sam{} Compare with Specialized Tools?}
\label{sec:exp:tools}

We compare \sam{} with the target-hidden pipelines of AutoSpec, SESpec,
Clause2Inv, Loopy, and Daikon~\citep{wen2024enchanting,yang2026sespec,
cao2025clause2inv,kamath2023finding,daikon}.  All LLM-based systems use
GPT-5 Nano, and all outputs are judged on the same untouched
programs by Frama-C/WP.  Each system retains its native, predeclared
orchestration, so model-call and token budgets differ;
Table~\ref{tab:tool-comparison} reports both token and runtime costs.
Unsupported inputs count as failures in the common 832-program denominator.

The Naive baseline uses one call without invariant-specific instructions.
In contrast, \sam{} prompts for a diverse clause pool and filters the result
before composition.  Consequently, its raw responses have lower standalone
validity than Naive (pass@1: 3.61\% vs.\ 16.47\%), while its filtered
composition reaches 33.89\% at the same one-response budget.

than strict equal-compute dominance; the matched Naive comparison isolates
the 17.4-point gain from one-response filtering and composition.

\subsection{RQ4: How Much Does Reinforcement Learning Improve Inference?}
\label{sec:exp:rl}

We evaluate matched Qwen3-8B before--after RL paths from the Zero and SFT
initializations: Zero is the untrained checkpoint and RL-Zero its RL
result; SFT and SFT+RL are the corresponding SFT-path checkpoints.
Inference is fixed within each pair.

\paragraph{What to expect.}
If RL with whole-response credit merely sharpens the
distribution~\citep{yue2025rlcapacity}, it should raise pass@1 while leaving
or degrading large-\(k\) coverage.  Clause-level credit with group
allocation predicts the opposite pattern: pass@\(k\) stays near the
initialization, while compose@\(k\) shifts up at small budgets without
degrading at large ones.  We write \(k^{*}\) for the smallest budget in the
measured grid at which the RL policy's compose@\(k\) reaches its
initialization's compose@32.
The SFT stage distills the filtered multi-response union into single
responses (Section~\ref{sec:method:sft}); the matched SFT-path row appears in
Table~\ref{tab:rl-before-after}, with full curves across the backbones in
Appendix~\ref{app:sft-probe}.

\subsection{RQ5: What Drives the Training Gain?}
\label{sec:exp:ablations}

We run two ablations, one over the reward function and one over the
negative sampler.  Both train Qwen3-8B from the Zero initialization on the
curated pool; runs within an ablation differ only in the reward or the
sampler.
Appendix~\ref{app:ablation-definitions} gives the exact reward definitions
and complete numbers.

\paragraph{Reward-function ablation.}
Four variants form a ladder.  Binary pays \(\{0,1\}\) when a nonempty
response has every admitted clause retained by the pooled group filter;
Whole-rollout pays \(\operatorname{cov}_i(\mathcal G)\) under the same condition;
Clause-decomposed pays it for the surviving subset
\(C_i^{\mathrm{attr}}(\mathcal G)\); Full adds the group (Shapley) credit
\(w_g\phi_i(\mathcal G)\) of Equation~\eqref{eq:generation-reward}.  The first two
are response-level credit, the last two clause-level; all share the clause
cap \(K{=}20\).

\paragraph{Insight.}
The reward unit determines whether RLVR sharpens responses or expands the
composable pool.  Whole-rollout credit raises pass@1 from 8.3\% to 25.4\%
but lowers compose@32 from 62.5\% to 33.2\%; binary credit reaches only
27.9\%.  Paying retained clauses instead raises compose@32 to 70.2\%, and
the fixed-game group bonus adds 4.1 points to 74.3\%.  Thus most of the gain
comes from removing the all-or-nothing response gate; inverse-frequency
group allocation supplies a smaller, consistent complementarity gain.

\paragraph{Negative-sampler ablation.}
Under the full reward, the structured sampler (relation and post-exit

\paragraph{Does negative coverage predict hidden-target utility?}
We score 6,190 saved target-hidden candidate sets from eight generation
pipelines on the structured negatives, then use the restored-target
Frama-C/WP verdict only as an evaluation label.  Among the 6,182 candidates
with a continuous coverage score, coverage predicts target success with
AUROC (area under the ROC curve) \(0.793\) (program-clustered 95\% CI
\(0.775\)--\(0.811\)) and AUPRC (area under the precision--recall curve)
\(0.768\), against a \(0.525\) positive-rate baseline.  More stringently,
ranking candidates only against other candidates for the same program gives
macro AUROC \(0.802\) (\(0.783\)--\(0.820\)); a within-program permutation
test gives \(p<2\times10^{-4}\).  The association is strong on Linear and
Loopy but weak on NLA, exposing a limitation of the current negative
construction.
Appendix~\ref{app:coverage-predictiveness} gives the protocol, strata, and
diagnostic curves.
